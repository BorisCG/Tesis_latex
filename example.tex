\chapter{Introduction}
\label{cap:1_Introduction}

One of the most enigmatic phenomena in astronomy is the existence of black holes. Since our understanding of the universe is based primarily on the observation of light, the discovery of objects with no emission is, at least, challenging. This lack of emission can be understood from a fundamental property of astronomical objects, the escape velocity. For any body sufficiently massive, it is possible to measure its gravitational acceleration:

\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{a}=\frac{GM}{r^2}~,
\end{equation}

where $M$ is the mass and $r$ the distance from the object. With this data is possible to calculate the velocity for escaping from their gravity, the escape velocity:

\begin{equation}
	v_{\rm esc} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}~.
\end{equation}

When an object is dense enough that the escape velocity at its surface exceeds the speed of light, it is classified as a black hole. However, black holes are not totally invisible, as their effect on the surrounding is detected.

Black holes are commonly classified according to their mass $M_{\rm BH}$. Stellar-mass black holes (BHs), typically with masses up to $\sim 100~M_{\odot}$, are thought to form from the collapse of massive stars, either through supernova explosions or via direct collapse \citep{belczynski2010maximum,o2011black}. On the other side are supermassive black holes (SMBHs), exhibiting masses in the range $10^6$--$10^{10}~M_{\odot}$ \citep{natarajan2009there,woods2019titans}. However, the mechanism of their formation remains an open question in astrophysics.

\section{Brief history: from Quasars to SMBHs}
\label{sec:1.1_SMBH_evidence}

By the 1960s, there was evidence accounting for star-like objects with radio sources. In 1962, three of the brightest radio sources were identified in the northern sky by Thomas Matthews and Allan Sandage: 3C 48, 3C 196 and 3C 286 \citep{garwin2019century}. The spectrum of 3C 48 was characterized by broad emission lines, and from photometry the object showed variability on timescales of months, and also an excess of ultraviolet emission, compared with normal stars \citep{shields1999brief,garwin2019century}. Those objects became known as quasi-stellar radio sources (QSRS), quasi-stellar sources (QSO), or quasars \citep{shields1999brief}. In 1963, \citet{greenstein1963red} identified emission lines in 3C 48 of Mg II, [Ne III], [Ne V], and [O II], with a redshift of $z=0.3675$. Interpreting this object as a galaxy core, they estimated a distance of $1.1 \times 10^9$ pc, and absolute magnitude (after redshift correction) of $-26.3$, making it at least ten times brighter than the brightest known elliptical galaxies. By the same year, \citet{schmidt19633c273} reported the observation of 3C 273. Schmidt noticed that the spectrum presented emission lines from hydrogen, shifted to longer wavelengths. He identified four emission bands as Balmer lines, $H\beta$, $H\gamma$, $H\delta$ and $H\epsilon$, with a redshift of $z=0.158$.

	\label{img:quasar_3C_273}

If these objects were stars in our Galaxy, the large redshift would have to be gravitational in origin, requiring extremely high densities. \citet{schmidt19633c273} estimated a radius on the order of $\sim 10~{\rm km}$ for such a compact object. The second explanation, which Schmidt considered the most direct and least objectionable, was that 3C 273 was the nucleus of a distant galaxy with a cosmological redshift, with a relative velocity of $47\,400~{\rm km}/{\rm sec}$, and a distance of $\sim 500~{\rm Mpc}$, and a diameter of $\lesssim 1~{\rm kpc}$.

The first suggestion that quasars are fueled by SMBHs came with the work of \citet{salpeter1965accretion}. Salpeter proposed that extremely massive objects could accrete interstellar gas. Given an object of mass $M$, velocity $v$ relative to a gas of density $n$ (H atoms per ${\rm cm}^3$), he defined a characteristic radius $s_0 = GM/v^2$ (from \citet{hoyle1939evolution}). Then, an accretion rate is defined:

\begin{equation}
	\frac{dM}{dt} = 2 \pi \alpha s_0^2 n v = \frac{\alpha M}{t_0}~,
	\label{eq:acc_hoyle}
\end{equation}

where $\alpha$ is a parameter that depends on the specific heat $\gamma$ of the gas. Salpeter also estimated that for the total mass being accreted per unit time, a fraction $f$ of 0.05--0.2 can escape as energy to infinity, while a fraction $1-f>0.8$ contribute to the increase in mass of the object. The optical luminosity is estimated as:

\begin{equation}
	L_{\rm opt} = \left(\frac{25}{v}\right)^{3} n \frac{\alpha}{0.25} \frac{f_{\rm opt}}{0.03} \left(\frac{M}{10^7}\right)^2 \times 2 \times 10^9 L_{\odot}~,
	\label{eq:lum_salpeter}
\end{equation}

where $L_{\odot}$ is the solar luminosity and $v$ is in ${\rm km}/{\rm s}$. In solar units, $L_{\rm opt}/M$ can grow as large as $3 \times 10^4$, then radiation pressure lowers $\alpha$, such that $L_{\rm opt}/M$ remains approximately constant.

The model was further developed and popularized by \citet{lynden1969galactic}, who argued that the remnants of quasars should be common in the centres of nearby galaxies. He proposed that most quasars should have consumed their fuel, on a timescale of $10^{10}$ years (the age of the universe). Those dead quasars should then be common in galactic centers. This is explained by the fact that as quasars consume the accreted gas, the Schwarzschild radius (the event horizon, a sphere of radius $r=2GM/c^2$ where the escape velocity equals the speed of light) becomes larger, consuming larger amounts of material. Also, in the past galaxies have larger amounts of gas than now.

One of the most important confirmations came with the discovery of a SMBH in the center of the Milky Way. In 1974, a compact radio source at the center of our galaxy, Sagittarius A\textsuperscript{*} (Sgr A\textsuperscript{*}), was discovered \citep{balick1974intense}. In the late 1990s, \citet{ghez1999high} studied the proper motions of 90 stars near the center. They found that the movements indicated that a dark mass of $2.6(\pm0.2) \times 10^6 ~M_{\odot}$ was confined to a volume of $10^{-6}~{\rm pc}^3$, coincident with Sgr A\textsuperscript{*}. They concluded that our Galaxy harbours a SMBH at its center. The definitive proof came years later. In 2017, the Event Horizon Telescope (EHT; \citet{doeleman2009imaging}), an interferometer based on Very Long Baseline Interferometry (VLBI), observed Sgr A\textsuperscript{*} \citep{event2022first}. They found that images of Sgr A\textsuperscript{*} were consistent with a Kerr black hole, with a mass of $\sim 4\times 10^6~M_{\odot}$, and a bright ring with a diameter of $51.8 \pm 2.3~\micro{\rm as}$, and a dim interior (see Figure~\ref{img:Sag_A}).

\section{Formation channels for SMBHs}
\label{sec:1.1_SMBH_formation_chanels}

The accumulated evidence for the existence of SMBHs suggests that they lie in the mass range of $\sim 10^6$--$10^{10}~M_{\odot}$ \citep{natarajan2009there,volonteri2010formation,woods2019titans,latif2016formation,gillessen2009orbit}. The origin of such dense and massive BHs is an active research topic in astronomy, as its principal formation mechanism remains unknown \citep{woods2019titans}. 

The challenge intensified with the discovery of quasars at redshifts $z>6$. Early surveys, such as the Canada-France High-z Quasar Survey (CFHQS), identified the first quasars beyond $z=6$ \citep{willott2007four}. Similarly, \citet{jiang2015discovery} reported eight quasars at $z\sim6$ from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS). More recently, \citet{yang2026euclid} reported the discovery of 31 new quasars at $6.6 < z < 7.8$ \citep{yang2026euclid} from the Euclid Wide Survey. Among them, EUCL J1729 is the most distant quasar discovered to date, with a redshift of $z \sim 7.77$, surpassing the quasar reported by \citet{mortlock2011luminous} at $z\sim 7.085$, and J0313−1806 at $z\sim7.6$ \citep{wang2021luminous}, which hosts a SMBH of $\sim 1.6 \times 10^9~M_{\odot}$. At $z \sim 7.77$, the age of the Universe was $\sim 662~{\rm Myr}$ \citep{yang2026euclid}, raising the natural question: how could SMBHs have formed at such early times?

To understand the formation pathways of SMBHs, it is important to know how they are seeded. \citet{regan2024massive} make the distinction between light and heavy seeds, where the former are objects with mass $< 1000~M_{\odot}$, and the latter are objects with mass $> 1000~M_{\odot}$. They estimate that light seeds accreting gas at a 'standard' radiative efficiency of $\epsilon_{r} \sim 0.1$, takes $\sim 1~{\rm Gyr}$ to grow up to a mass of $\sim 10^9~M_{\odot}$. However, those seeds are unlikely to grow efficiently enough. \citet{alvarez2009accretion} showed that for black hole remnant of Population III stars photoionization and heating affect the inflow of gas, resulting in negligible mass growth of the seed, making them unlikely to produce the observed quasars at $z>6$. In agreement, another study of  a simulation of $15\,000$ black holes remnants of Population III stars, over $300~{\rm Myr}$, showed that fewer than 100 BHs grew by more than $10^{-3}$ of their initial masses, most of them coming from dense regions \citep{smith2018growth}. Heavy seeds, however, can be formed by runaway collisions of dense star clusters \citep{escala2021observational,portegies2004formation}, or the formation of a supermassive star (SMS) of $\gtrsim 10^5~M_{\odot}$  \citep{woods2017maximum}.

Although heavy seeds are more likely to grow into SMBHs, they require rarer environmental conditions, resulting in a lower number density. For a redshift of $z\sim 10$, the heaviest seeds, with masses up to $\sim 10^5~M_{\odot}$, have number densities of $\sim 10^{-10}$--$10^{-5}~{\rm cMpc}^{-3}$, while other heavy seeds have number densities of $\sim 10^{-10}$--$10^{-1}~{\rm cMpc}^{-3}$. In contrast, light seeds are more common, with a number density of $\sim 10^{-1}$--$10^{1}~{\rm cMpc}^{-3}$ \citep{regan2024massive}.

	\label{img:light_heavy_seeds}

There are many pathways for the formation of SMBHs \citep{volonteri2010formation}. In the following, we describe three channels: (i) Population III stars remnant, (ii) direct collapse black hole, and (iii) runaway stellar collisions in dense star clusters.

\subsection{Population III stars}

One of the pathways of SMBH formation is through remnants of Population III stars. Simulations have shown that Population III stars form in halos of $10^5$--$10^6~M_{\odot}$, and since fragmentation is typically suppressed under these conditions, they form in isolation at $z \sim 17$ \citep{o2007population,o2008population}. After $\sim 3~{\rm Myr}$, those stars can end in a supernova explosion or via direct collapse, leaving a BH remnant of $\sim 10^2~M_{\odot}$ \citep{fryer2001pair}. However, their subsequent growth into the SMBH mass range seems unlikely. In an optimistic scenario, \citet{vesperini2010intermediate} showed that the gas ejected by first-generation (Population III) stars can explain the chemical abundances in second generation (SG) stars, and that this dense gas can lead to significant accretion onto a pre-existing BH at the Eddington rate, increasing the mass by up to a factor of 100 ($\sim 10^4~M_{\odot}$). They estimated that a BH mass of $\gtrsim 10^4 ~M_{\odot}$ is required for the wind produced by the black hole to expel the accreting gas. However, simulations show that a $100~M_{\odot}$ BH accreting metal-free gas exhibits intermittent accretion, which is on average reduced to $\sim 32\%$ of the Eddington rate \citep{milosavljevic2009accretion}. In consequence, the mass increase is reduced to a factor of $\sim2$--$5$ \citep{vesperini2010intermediate}. Other simulations also report inefficient accretion onto Pop III BH remnants, due to the erratic dynamics of these BHs within their host clusters \citep{smith2018growth,pfister2019erratic}. However, if Pop III stars form in dense clusters rather than in isolation, runaway collisions can lead to the formation of heavier seeds ($\sim 10^3$--$10^4~M_{\odot}$), as we discuss later.

\subsection{Direct collapse of primordial gas clouds}

Another pathway is the formation of a heavy seed of $\sim 10^5~M_{\odot}$, via the direct collapse of primordial gas clouds. A set of simulations performed by \citet{bromm2003formation} studied the evolution of a gas cloud with a total mass of $10^8~M_{\odot}$ (including dark matter and baryons), varying the spin parameter $\lambda$, the inclusion of H$_2$ cooling, and the presence of a photo-dissociating Lyman-Werner (LW) background. They found that when H$_2$ cooling is suppressed, the gas collapses towards the centre via atomic hydrogen cooling, without fragmenting. Their simulations showed that $\gtrsim 10^6~M_{\odot}$ of the gas condenses into a region of $\lesssim 1~{\rm pc}$, after which a supermassive star is likely to form. This SMS could subsequently collapse into a BH, retaining approximately $90\%$ of its mass \citep{baumgarte1999evolution,shibata2002collapse}. However, the presence of H$_2$ cools the gas efficiently, leading to the formation of Population III stars. Their short lives ($\sim 3~{\rm Myr}$) produce supernova events (SNe) that enrich the gas, favouring subsequent star formation. To suppress H$_2$ cooling, the collapsing cloud must be exposed to a strong Lyman-Werner flux, typically provided by a neighbouring star-forming halo. This seeding mechanism requires very specific conditions, making it difficult to explain the observed number density of quasars at $z>6$. Although \citet{shang2010supermassive} found that the number of halos capable of suppressing H$_2$ formation may be larger, the subsequent mass growth of a $\sim 10^5~M_{\odot}$ seed faces significant challenges in sustaining the necessary accretion rates, as demonstrated by \citet{chon2021cosmological}.

\subsection{Runaway collisions in dense star clusters}
\label{sec:Intro_Runaway_coll}

As discussed in the previous section, when H$_2$ cooling is efficient, the gas can fragment, leading to the formation of Population III stars \citep{abel2000formation,liao2019fragmentation}. As more massive halos form, metal enrichment from Population III stars can lead to fragmentation and the formation of low-mass stars \citep{volonteri2010formation,omukai2008can}. This opens the possibility of another scenario for the formation of SMBH seeds: runaway collisions in dense stellar clusters. 

As the cluster evolves, mass segregation drives heavy stars to the center and light stars to the outer regions, through dynamical friction and two-body relaxation. This tendency towards equipartition of kinetic energy can lead to the gravothermal instability \citep{1987degc.book.....S}. In this scenario, the core may undergo a runaway collision process, leading to the formation of a massive black hole \citep{gurkan2004formation,rasio2003formation,escala2021observational}. This scenario has been tested through N-body simulations of nuclear star clusters (NSCs) \citep{das2021formation,vergara2023global,vergara2024efficiency,vergara2025rapid,portegies2002runaway,portegies2004formation,sakurai2017formation}, as well as young Population III star clusters \citep{freitag2006runaway_a,freitag2006runaway_b,vergara2021stellar,reinoso2020effects,reinoso2023formation}, showing the formation of a seed with mass $\sim 10^3$--$10^4~M_{\odot}$.

Most of these simulations consider purely gravitational interactions in gas-free clusters. However, gas can significantly enhance the formation of an SMBH seed. Gas damping can significantly accelerate mass segregation and drive overall cluster contraction \citep{leigh2014stellar}. Gas accretion also contributes to cluster contraction \citep{bonnell2002accretion,reinoso2024formation}, and can subsequently fuel growth at the Eddington rate \citep{chung2026dense}. Dynamical friction from star-gas interactions can also drive massive stars towards the centre \citep{ostriker1999dynamical,leigh2014stellar}, however, this requires sufficient resolution to be adequately captured in simulations.

\chapter{A scenario for SMBH formation}
\label{chap:2_SMBH_formation_scenario}

In this section, we describe the scenario proposed by \citet{escala2021observational} extended to include an external potential. The scenario explores the role of collisional and relaxation timescales ($t_{\rm coll}$ and $t_{\rm relax}$) in determining the global stability of stellar systems. 

As stars move in a cluster, gravitational interactions redistribute kinetic and potential energy through two-body encounters. Strong encounters can lead to the formation or disruption of binaries, the ejection of stars, or stellar mergers when stars physically overlap \citep{2008gady.book.....B,1987degc.book.....S}. Mass segregation drives massive stars toward the centre, increasing the central density and accelerating core collapse. When the density becomes sufficiently high, stellar collisions become frequent, potentially triggering a runaway phase that can lead to a global instability \citep{escala2021observational}. 

Weak encounters produce small, gradual changes in stellar velocities, but their cumulative effect leads to two-body relaxation, which drives mass segregation and can bring the system toward a state of partial energy equipartition \citep{trenti2013no,bianchini2016novel}. The relevant timescales for describing these processes are the collisional timescale ($t_{\rm coll}$) and the relaxation timescale ($t_{\rm relax}$).

The collisional timescale $t_{\rm coll}$ provides an estimate of the collision rate between particles in a system. For a stellar system of identical stars, a first estimate of the collision rate $1/t_{\rm coll}$ is given by \citet{2008gady.book.....B}:

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:coll_rate}
	\frac{1}{t_{\rm coll}} = 16\sqrt{\pi} n \sigma r_{\star}^2(1 + \Theta)~,
\end{equation}

where $r_{\star}$ is the stellar radius, $\sigma$ is the velocity dispersion of the system, $n$ is the number density of stars, and

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:safronov_number}
	\Theta \equiv \frac{v_{\star}^2}{4 \sigma^2} = 9.54 \frac{m}{M_{\odot}} \frac{R_{\odot}}{r_{\star}} \left(\frac{100~{\rm km}~{\rm s}^{-1}}{\sigma}\right)^2~,
\end{equation}

is the Safronov number, with $m$ the mass of each star and $v_{\star} = \sqrt{2Gm/r_{\star}}$ the escape speed from the stellar surface. This derivation assumes a Maxwellian velocity distribution with dispersion $\sigma$. Equation~\eqref{eq:coll_rate} gives the global collision rate for the system, from typical stellar properties ($m$, $r_{\star}$, and $\sigma$).

The other relevant timescale is the relaxation time. As a system evolves, two-body relaxation can alter its initial conditions. Close encounters and mass segregation redistribute energy and mass, changing the system's initial global properties (e.g., radius and velocity dispersion). Although strong encounters can significantly change a star's velocity, they are less frequent than weak encounters. The relaxation timescale $t_{\rm relax}$ is the time required for a typical star to change its velocity significantly through the cumulative effect of weak encounters. From \citet{2008gady.book.....B},

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:trelax}
	t_{\rm relax} = n_{\rm relax}t_{\rm cross},
\end{equation}

where $t_{\rm cross}$ is the crossing time of a typical star, and $n_{\rm relax}$ is the number of crossings needed for the cumulative change in velocity $\delta v$ to become comparable to the star's velocity ($dv = \sum \delta v \sim v$). For a system of $N$ identical stars, $n_{\rm relax} \sim 0.1N/\log N$ (see Appendix~\ref{sec:app_Mrelax}). Thus,

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:trelax_2}
	t_{\rm relax} = \frac{0.1N}{\log(N)}t_{\rm cross}.
\end{equation}

\section{Critical mass}
\label{sec:2.1_Mcrit}

The relevance of the relaxation and collision timescales depends on the characteristic timescale of the system, $t_{\rm H}$, typically taken as the age of the system. If $t_{\rm coll} \lesssim t_{\rm H}$, collisions are expected to be dynamically relevant. For a virialized system of total mass $M$ and radius $R$, Equation~\eqref{eq:coll_rate} can be rearranged \citep{escala2021observational}:

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:tcoll_escala}
	t_{\rm coll} = \frac{1}{n \Sigma_0}\sqrt{\frac{R}{GM}}~,
\end{equation}

where $\Sigma_0=16\sqrt{\pi}r_{\star}^2(1+\Theta)$ is the effective cross-section, and $\sigma = \sqrt{GM/R}$ for a virialized system. The Safronov number can be simplified to $\Theta \sim 9.54 (m/M_{\odot})(R_{\odot}/r_{\star})$ if we assume $\sigma \sim 100~{\rm km}~{\rm s}^{-1}$. Assuming a homogeneous density distribution as a first approximation, the number density is $n=3M/(4\pi R^3m)$. Imposing $t_{\rm coll} \leq t_{\rm H}$ and solving for the mass yields a critical mass \citep{escala2021observational,vergara2023global}:

\begin{equation}
	M \geq \left(\frac{4 \pi m}{3 \Sigma_0 t_{\rm H} G^{1/2}}\right)^{2/3} R^{7/3}=M_{\rm crit}~.
\end{equation}

The condition $t_{\rm coll} \leq t_{\rm H}$ is equivalent to $M \geq M_{\rm crit}$. Thus, $M_{\rm crit}$ is the mass of a virialized system of radius $R$ and equal-mass stars whose collision timescale equals $t_{\rm coll} = t_{\rm H}$. Including gravitational focusing and an external potential of mass $M_{\rm ext} = qM$, the critical mass can be written as (see Appendix~):

\begin{equation}
	M_{\rm crit} = \left(\frac{\beta R^{7/2}}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{\beta^2 R^7}{4} + \frac{\alpha^3 R^3}{27}}\right)^{2/3} + \left(\frac{\beta R^{7/2}}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{\beta^2 R^7}{4} + \frac{\alpha^3 R^3}{27}}\right)^{2/3} - \frac{2\alpha R}{3}~,
	\label{eq:Mcrit_ext}
\end{equation}

with

\begin{align}
	\alpha &= \frac{9.54 ~ mR_\odot v_{100}^2}{r_\star G M_\odot (1+q)^2} \\
	\beta &= \frac{\sqrt{\pi} m}{12 G^{1/2}t_H r_\star^2 (1+q)}~. 
\end{align}

\section{Relaxation mass}
\label{sec:2.1_Mrelax}

Similarly, we can obtain a condition using the relaxation time. From Appendix~\ref{sec:app_Mrelax}, including an external potential, the relaxation timescale (Equation~\eqref{eq:trelax_ext}) yields

\begin{equation}
	t_{\rm relax} = \frac{0.1 N(1+q)^3}{\ln(N(1+q)^2)} \frac{R^{3/2}}{\sqrt{GM}}~.
\end{equation}

Replacing $N=M/m$, and solving $t_{\rm relax} \leq t_{\rm H}$ for the characteristic radius $R$:

\begin{equation}
	R \leq \left(\frac{t_{\rm H}m}{0.1(1+q)^3} \ln(\frac{M(1+q)^2}{m})\right)^{2/3} \left(\frac{G}{M}\right)^{1/3}~.
	\label{eq:r_relax}
\end{equation}

This equation gives a condition on the radius such that the system evolves significantly due to two-body relaxation over $t_{\rm H}$. We can rewrite Equation~\eqref{eq:r_relax} by solving for the mass,

\begin{equation}
	M \leq M_{\rm relax} = \frac{m}{(1+q)^2}\exp(-2 W_{-1}\left(\frac{-1}{2\lambda}\right))~,
	\label{eq:M_relax_ext}
\end{equation}

where $W_{-1}(z)$ is the lower branch of the Lambert function, and $\lambda = (t_{\rm H}\sqrt{Gm})/(0.1(1+q)^2R^{3/2})$. In such systems, two-body relaxation does not significantly alter the initial conditions over the characteristic timescale $t_{\rm H}$. If, in addition, $t_{\rm coll} \ll t_{\rm H}$, the system remains approximately in virial equilibrium. The case where $t_{\rm coll} \leq t_{\rm H}$ despite $t_{\rm relax} \geq t_{\rm H}$ corresponds to a different regime, which we discuss in the following section.

\section{Global instability scenario}
\label{sec:instability_scenario}

In this section, we analyze the scenario for supermassive black hole (SMBH) formation from NSCs proposed by \citet{escala2021observational}. The critical mass $M_{\rm crit}$ from Equation~\eqref{eq:Mcrit_ext} gives a threshold for the condition $t_{\rm coll} = t_{\rm H}$. For stellar clusters where $t_{\rm coll} \leq t_{\rm H}$ ($M \geq M_{\rm crit}$), the onset of runaway collisions is expected, eventually forming a BH at the centre.  On the other hand, when $t_{\rm relax} \leq t_{\rm H}$ (which is equivalent to Equation~\eqref{eq:M_relax_ext}, where $M_{\rm relax}$ is the limiting case), two-body interactions could expand the cluster, modifying its initial conditions. \citet{escala2021observational} proposed a new regime of global instability, given by $t_{\rm coll} \leq t_{\rm H} \leq t_{\rm relax}$. This regime has two key aspects. First, since $t_{\rm coll} \leq t_{\rm H}$, collisions occur within the characteristic timescale $t_{\rm H}$. Second, the condition $t_{\rm coll} \leq t_{\rm relax}$ implies that two-body relaxation is unable to reverse the global collapse. As a result, the onset of runaway collisions leads to the formation of a massive black hole (MBH). A second scenario can also lead to the formation of a central BH. We define an intermediate regime, given by clusters where $t_{\rm relax} \leq t_{\rm coll} \leq t_{\rm H}$. In this regime, the system could begin to expand on a timescale comparable to $t_{\rm relax}$. However, this does not necessarily prevent the onset of a collisional runaway in the core. Since the central density is significantly higher than the global average, the local collision time $t_{\rm coll, core}$ is considerably shorter than the global collision time $t_{\rm coll}$. If $t_{\rm coll, core} \leq t_{\rm relax, core}$, the core could contract and decouple dynamically from the expanding envelope, which could be compatible with a gravothermal instability scenario \citep{1987degc.book.....S, cohn1980late}. Then, the core could undergo runaway collisions, forming a central BH coexisting with a surrounding star cluster, while the outer stars, heated by two-body relaxation and dynamical encounters, expand. Finally, a stable regime is defined by $t_{\rm relax} \leq t_{\rm H} \leq t_{\rm coll}$. In this stable regime, clusters can expand due to two-body relaxation, which further decreases the collision rate. Although a BH can form via collisions in the core, most of the mass could remain in the stellar cluster.

	\centering

The final fate of the CMO is conveniently explained in terms of the black hole formation efficiency, defined as $\epsilon_{\rm BH} = M_{\rm BH}/M_{\rm CMO}$ \citep{escala2021observational,vergara2023global}, where $M_{\rm BH}$ is the mass of the central black hole, and $M_{\rm CMO} = M_{\rm BH} + M_{\rm NSC}$ is the mass of the central massive object, composed of a central BH and/or a surrounding nuclear stellar cluster. In Figure~\ref{img:Collisional_regime}, critical mass and relaxation mass are displayed (dashed and solid curves, respectively), for solar-mass clusters. Two characteristic timescales are used, with $t_{\rm H} = 5 \times 10^6~{\rm yr}$ for blue curves, and $t_{\rm H} = 10^{10}~{\rm yr}$ for red curves. The stable and unstable regimes are separated at the intersection of the solid, dashed and black dot-dashed lines, where $t_{\rm coll} = t_{\rm relax} = t_{\rm H}$. The collisional regime (CR), characterized by $t_{\rm coll} \leq t_{\rm H} \leq t_{\rm relax}$, is in the zone where the mass of the system is simultaneously larger than the critical and the relaxation mass. In this regime, the system is unable to expand via two-body relaxation before collisions become relevant. Although binary heating can, in principle, oppose core contraction, in systems with masses larger than $10^7~M_{\odot}$ binary heating cannot reverse core collapse, and the system can suffer a merger instability, which may lead to the formation of a central BH \citep{lee1987dynamical}. The dissipative nature of collisions thus drives a net contraction of the cluster. Then, most of the mass could remain in the central BH, having efficiencies $\epsilon_{\rm BH} \sim 1$. In this regime, we expect systems to move towards the bottom-left of the diagram, as the core contracts and stars are ejected from the core. In contrast, in the stable regime the dynamics are dominated by two-body relaxation over collisions. In those systems, the mass occupies the region where $M \leq M_{\rm crit}$ and $M \leq M_{\rm relax}$ simultaneously. The expansion of the cluster moves it to the right in the M--R diagram, potentially crossing the solid line. Although a central black hole could form, most of the mass remains in the stellar cluster, resulting in an NSC. Consequently, efficiencies are $\epsilon_{\rm BH} \ll 1$. The intermediate regime (IR) corresponds to the region where $t_{\rm relax} \leq t_{\rm coll} \leq t_{\rm H}$. In the diagram, this is the area where $M_{\rm crit} \leq M \leq M_{\rm relax}$. As described above, the system could expand while the core undergoes a runaway collision process, forming a central BH. These systems eventually move to the right as their radius increases, crossing into the stable regime (right side of the dashed curve), with efficiencies spanning a wider range $\epsilon_{\rm BH} \leq 1$. As noted by \citet{escala2021observational}, the observed BH formation efficiency as a function of $M_{\rm CMO}$ shows a step-like form that clearly separates NSCs from SMBHs. A possible explanation for this observational gap is that systems in the intermediate regime may form IMBHs; however, an important fraction of the mass could remain in the surrounding stellar cluster. Consequently, their gravitational sphere of influence is small and challenging to resolve \citep{trenti2006dynamical}, making them difficult to detect. This would naturally contribute to the lack of IMBH detections in NSC-dominated systems.

\chapter{Numerical Methods}
\label{cap:3_Numerical_Methods}

In this chapter, we describe the numerical methods employed to model the coupled evolution of stars and gas in dense stellar clusters. We require three principal components: (i) an N-body code (\texttt{ph4}) to model stellar dynamics, (ii) a smoothed particle hydrodynamics (SPH) code (\texttt{Fi}) to simulate the gas, and (iii) a coupling scheme (\texttt{BRIDGE}) to handle their mutual gravitational interaction. The N-body and SPH codes used are accessed through the Astrophysical Multipurpose Software Environment (AMUSE; \citet{zwart2018astrophysical}), which provides a modular framework for solving multi-physics problems. We do not include gas accretion onto stars, as our focus is exclusively on the gravitational interaction between the two components.

\section{N-body code: PH4}
\label{sec:3.2_NBody}

The proposed scenario presented in Section~\ref{chap:2_SMBH_formation_scenario} requires high numerical precision to accurately model close encounters and direct collisions. We therefore employ the \texttt{ph4} N-body code from the AMUSE framework \citep{zwart2018astrophysical}. In this section, we include a description of the code, and the external routines implemented to handle collisions.

\subsection{Hermite integrator}
\label{subsec:3.2.1_Hermite}

The \texttt{ph4} code uses a fourth-order Hermite integrator \citep{makino1992} to compute the positions and velocities of stars under gravity. This predictor-corrector method employs adaptive individual time steps, ensuring high accuracy in regions of strong gravitational interactions. Considering a particle $i$ with its own time $t_i$, time step $\Delta t_i$, position $\mathbf{x}_i$, velocity $\mathbf{v}_i$, acceleration $\mathbf{a}_i$ and jerk $\dot{\mathbf{a}}_i$, all calculated at time $t_i$. The integration algorithm proceeds as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item Select the particle with the minimum $t_i + \Delta t_i$. Set the global time to $t=t_i + \Delta t_i$.
	\item Calculate the predicted positions ($\mathbf{x}_p$) and velocities ($\mathbf{v}_p$) for all particles, using the current values for $\mathbf{x}$, $\mathbf{v}$, $\mathbf{a}$, $\dot{\mathbf{a}}$. 
	\item Calculate the acceleration ($\mathbf{a}_i$) and jerk ($\dot{\mathbf{a}}_i$) for particle $i$ at time $t_i + \Delta t_i$ using the predicted positions and velocities.
	\item Calculate the second and third time derivatives of acceleration ($\mathbf{a}_i^{(2)}$ and $\mathbf{a}_i^{(3)}$) using Hermite interpolation.
	\item Apply corrections to the position and velocity of particle $i$.
	\item Calculate and update the time step $\Delta t_i$.
	\item Repeat the algorithm.
\end{enumerate}

The interpolation uses the acceleration and jerk at the beginning and end of the time step, allowing the scheme to achieve fourth-order accuracy without explicitly computing higher-order derivatives. A detailed derivation of the algorithm and the full set of equations is provided in Appendix~\ref{ap:A}. From Eqs.~(\ref{eq:ap_xpred}) and (\ref{eq:ap_velpred}), the predicted positions and velocities are:

\begin{align}
	\mathbf{x}_{p, j} &= \mathbf{x}_j + \mathbf{v}_j (t - t_j) + \frac{\mathbf{a}_{0,j}}{2} (t - t_j)^2 + \frac{\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{0,j}}{3!} (t - t_j)^3 \\
	\mathbf{v}_{p, j} &= \mathbf{v}_j + \mathbf{a}_{0,j} (t - t_j) + \frac{\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{0,j}}{2!} (t - t_j)^2
\end{align}

Here, $\mathbf{x}_j$ and $\mathbf{v}_j$ are the position and velocity of particle $j$ at time $t_j$. The current acceleration $\mathbf{a}_{0,j}$ and jerk $\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{0,j}$ are computed as:

 
\begin{align}
	\mathbf{a}_{0, j} &= \sum_{k \neq j} G m_k \frac{\mathbf{r}_{0,jk}}{(\mathbf{r}_{0,jk}^2 + \epsilon^2)^{3/2}}\\
	\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{0, j} &= \sum_{k \neq j} G m_k \left[\frac{\mathbf{v}_{0,jk}}{(\mathbf{r}_{0,jk}^2 + \epsilon^2)^{3/2}} - \frac{3(\mathbf{v}_{0,jk} \cdot \mathbf{r}_{0,jk})\mathbf{r}_{0,jk}}{(\mathbf{r}_{0,jk}^2 + \epsilon^2)^{5/2}}\right] \\
	\mathbf{r}_{0,jk} &= \mathbf{r}_{k} - \mathbf{r}_{j} \\
	\mathbf{v}_{0,jk} &= \mathbf{v}_{k} - \mathbf{v}_{j}
\end{align}

Using Hermite interpolation, the second and third time derivatives of the acceleration can be estimated. This estimation is subsequently used to correct the positions and velocities. The relevant expressions, derived in the appendix, are:

\begin{align}
	\mathbf{a}^{(2)}_{0, i} &= \frac{-6(\mathbf{a}_{0, i} - \mathbf{a}_{1, i}) - \Delta t_i(4\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{0, i} + 2\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{1, i})}{{\Delta t_i}^2} \\
	\mathbf{a}^{(3)}_{0, i} &= \frac{12 (\mathbf{a}_{0, i} - \mathbf{a}_{1, i}) + 6(\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{0, i} + \dot{\mathbf{a}}_{1, i})\Delta t_i}{{\Delta t_i}^3}
\end{align}

The final corrected position and velocity of particle $i$ are then given by:

\begin{align}
	\mathbf{x}_i(t_i + \Delta t_i) =& \mathbf{x}_{p, i} + \frac{\mathbf{a}^{(2)}_{0, i} {\Delta t_i}^4}{24} + \frac{\mathbf{a}^{(3)}_{0, i} {\Delta t_i}^5}{120} \\
	\mathbf{v}_i(t_i + \Delta t_i) =& \mathbf{v}_{p, i} + \frac{\mathbf{a}^{(2)}_{0, i} {\Delta t_i}^3}{6} + \frac{\mathbf{a}^{(3)}_{0, i} {\Delta t_i}^4}{24}
\end{align}

These corrections incorporate terms up to fourth order. The main advantage of this method is that it only requires the explicit calculation of the acceleration and jerk at each step, with higher-order derivatives being obtained through the interpolation scheme.

\subsection{Block time steps}
\label{subsec:3.2.2_Block_Tstep}

While the Hermite integrator computes individual time steps for each particle, evolving particles one by one is computationally expensive. \texttt{ph4} therefore groups particles into block time steps, which are powers of two of the N-body time unit. This allows particles with the same time step to be integrated simultaneously, improving efficiency while maintaining synchronization. The choice of time step directly affects the accuracy of the integration, the conservation of energy, and the total computational cost.

In the block scheme, time steps are set in two steps. First, a dynamic time step is calculated. Then, the time step is assigned using a block scheme. For the dynamical time step, the standard formula (\ref{eq:ap_tstep_std}) is used:

\begin{align}
	\Delta t_i = \sqrt{\eta \frac{|\mathbf{a}_{1, i}| |\mathbf{a}^{(2)}_{1, i}| + |\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{1, i}|^2}{|\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{1, i}| |\mathbf{a}^{(3)}_{1, i}| + |\mathbf{a}^{(2)}_{1, i}|^2}} ~. \label{eq:3.11}
\end{align}

As can be noted, $\mathbf{a}_{1, i}$ and $\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{1, i}$ are calculated in each step, while $\mathbf{a}^{(3)}_{1, i}$ is constant. Only $\mathbf{a}^{(2)}_{1, i}$ needs to be estimated:

\begin{align}
	\mathbf{a}^{(2)}_{1, i} = \mathbf{a}^{(2)}_{0, i} + \Delta t_i \mathbf{a}^{(3)}_{1, i}~.
\end{align}

When the simulation begins, the initial time steps are calculated using (\ref{eq:ap_tstep_ini}):

\begin{align}
	\Delta t = \eta_s \frac{|\mathbf{a}|}{|\dot{\mathbf{a}}|}~. \label{eq:3.13}
\end{align}

To avoid the computational cost of evolving particles individually, \texttt{ph4} assigns particles to discrete block time steps that are powers of two of the N-body time unit. Given the initial time step $\Delta t_{0, j}$ (calculated from Equation~\eqref{eq:3.13}), and the N-body time $\tau$, an integer $n$ is adjusted to satisfy the condition:

\begin{equation}
	0.5 < \frac{\tau 2^{n}}{\Delta t_{0, j}} < 1~.
\end{equation}

The initial time step of particles is then set to $\tau 2^{n}$, where $n$ can be positive or negative. As the model evolves, the new time step is computed using (\ref{eq:3.11}). The time step is halved if the calculated time step is smaller than the current time step, and doubled if it is larger than twice the current time step. The advantage of this time step criterion is that the time step in the code is equal to or lower than that required by the dynamics. Moreover, since particles share the same time steps, they can be integrated simultaneously. Additionally, all particles can be synchronized, as their times are multiples of powers of two of the same time unit.

\subsection{Collisions and mergers}
\label{subsec:3.2.3_Collisions_mergers}

Stellar collisions are detected through a two-stage filter. First, \texttt{ph4} checks whether two particles are closer than the sum of their radii: $|\Delta \mathbf{x}| < r_i + r_j$. A second filter then verifies close encounters using the criterion $ \epsilon |\Delta \mathbf{x}| |\Delta \mathbf{v}| > \Delta \mathbf{x} \cdot \Delta \mathbf{v}$, where $\epsilon = 0.001$. This avoids false detections caused by hyperbolic encounters with predominantly tangential relative velocities. Once a collision is detected, the flag \texttt{collision\_detection} in the code is activated, and a custom handling routine is executed. In our implementation, the two particles involved in the collision are identified as particles $i$ and $j$, corresponding to the most and less massive between them, respectively. The properties of particle $i$ are then modified: its mass is set to the sum of the masses, its position to the centre of mass, its velocity to the centre-of-mass velocity, and its radius is updated to conserve mass density:

\begin{align}
	m_{i, \text{new}} &= m_i + m_j \\
	\mathbf{x}_{i, \text{new}} &= \frac{m_i \mathbf{x}_{i} + m_j \mathbf{x}_{j}}{m_i + m_j} \\
	\mathbf{v}_{i, \text{new}} &= \frac{m_i \mathbf{v}_{i} + m_j \mathbf{v}_{j}}{m_i + m_j} \\
	r_{i, \text{new}} &= r_i \left(\frac{m_i + m_j}{m_i}\right)^{1/3}~. \\	
\end{align}

Finally, particle $j$ is removed from the simulation.

\subsection{Escaping stars}
\label{subsec:3.2.4_Escapers}

After each time step, potential escapers are identified through a multi-stage detection algorithm. To maintain mass and energy conservation, escaped stars are not removed from the simulation. Instead, we add an extra attribute, \texttt{escaped} (all stars begin with \texttt{particles.escaped = False}), to track bound and unbound stars for subsequent analysis. 

To detect escaping stars, the first step is to define an appropriate length scale. In the initialization, the escape scale is defined as the minimum between ten times the virial radius and the Lagrangian radius containing the total mass: 

\begin{equation}
	r_{0, \text{esc}} = \min (10 \times r_{\text{vir}}, r_{100})
\end{equation}

where $r_{100}$ represents the Lagrangian radius containing the total stellar mass. In subsequent iterations, the escape distance is updated as:

\begin{equation}
	r_{\text{esc}} = \max (r_{\text{esc}}, 10 \times r_{b, \text{vir}})
\end{equation}

where $r_{b, \text{vir}}$ denotes the virial radius of bound stars. The escape detection algorithm, executed at the end of each time step, proceeds as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item Compute the core center and identify stars beyond the escape radius.
	\item Select gravitationally unbound stars.
	\item Identify stars with outward radial motion.
	\item Verify minimal gravitational influence on nearest neighbor.
\end{enumerate}

The core centre location is determined using a density-weighted scheme following \citet{1993MNRAS.261..497S}. For each star, the distance to its 7th nearest neighbor ($r_{i,7}$) is computed, and the density center is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{x}_c = \frac{\sum_{i = 1}^{N} \mathbf{x}_i / r_{i,7}^3}{\sum_{i = 1}^{N} 1 / r_{i,7}^3} \label{eq:3.24}
\end{equation}

This formulation resembles a center of mass calculation but uses local stellar density instead of mass. Stars satisfying the condition

\begin{equation}
	|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_c| > r_{\text{esc}}
\end{equation}

are identified as potential escapers. Gravitational unbinding is evaluated using the total energy $E_i = E_{p,i} + E_{k,i}$, where $E_{p,i}$ is the gravitational potential energy (including contributions from both stars and gas) and $E_{k,i}$ is the kinetic energy. Stars with $E_i \leq 0$ are considered bound, while those with $E_i > 0$ are classified as unbound. Among unbound stars, outward-moving candidates are identified through the condition:

\begin{equation}
	\Delta\mathbf{x}_i \cdot \Delta\mathbf{v}_i > 0
\end{equation}

where $\Delta\mathbf{x}_i = \mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_c$ and $\Delta\mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{v}_i - \mathbf{v}_c$ represent position and velocity relative to the cluster center. Similar to (\ref{eq:3.24}), the velocity of the core center is computed as

\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{v}_c = \frac{\sum_{i = 1}^{N} \mathbf{v}_i m_i / r_{i,7}^3}{\sum_{i = 1}^{N} m_i / r_{i,7}^3}
\end{equation}

The final validation step quantifies the gravitational influence on neighboring stars. For each candidate escaper, the relative potential energy ($e_{p,i}$) and kinetic energy ($e_{k,i}$) with respect to its nearest neighbor are computed. The energy ratio is defined as:

\begin{equation}
	X_E := \frac{|e_{p,i}|}{e_{k,i}}
\end{equation}

Stars satisfying $X_E < 0.01$ are confirmed as escapers, ensuring minimal gravitational perturbation to neighboring stars. These stars are tagged as escapers (\texttt{particles.escaped = True}). Tagged escapers are excluded from subsequent dynamical calculations (e.g., core radius, density profiles), but they are not removed from the simulation, ensuring mass and energy conservation.

\section{SPH code: Fi}
\label{sec:3.3}

The gas dynamics are modelled using the \texttt{Fi} code \citep{gerritsen1997star}, an MPI-parallel smoothed particle hydrodynamics (SPH) implementation within the AMUSE framework. We focus on the aspects most relevant to our simulations; a comprehensive description of the code can be found in \citet{pelupessy2005numerical}.

\subsection{Barnes-Hut tree}
\label{subsec:3.3.1_BHTree}


A key feature of \texttt{Fi} is its ability to compute the self-gravity of the gaseous component. Particle-based codes offer a range of methods for gravitational force calculation. The most accurate approach is the direct summation of all pairwise interactions, which scales as $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ and is computationally expensive for large $N$. While such precision is often necessary for collisional stellar dynamics (e.g., in our N-body component), the gas system in our simulations is treated as a collisionless fluid for the purpose of gravity calculation, allowing for faster, approximate methods, sacrificing accuracy for computational efficiency.

\texttt{Fi} employs the Barnes-Hut (BH) tree algorithm \citep{barnes1986hierarchical}. This method uses a hierarchical oct-tree spatial decomposition and a geometric opening criterion to approximate gravitational forces.

The tree is constructed by enclosing all particles within a root cell. If this cell contains more than one particle, it is subdivided into eight daughter cells of equal volume. This subdivision process repeats recursively for each cell containing multiple particles, until every cell holds at most one particle (a leaf node). Figure~\ref{img:oct_tree} illustrates this structure in two dimensions.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Esquemas/BHtree_plot.png}
	\caption{Representation of an oct tree}
	\label{img:oct_tree}
\end{figure}

Once the tree is built, the gravitational force on a given particle is calculated by going through the tree from the root. For each node encountered (linked to a cell), a decision is made: either open the node to examine its children, or accept the node as a single interacting entity. The classic Barnes-Hut opening criterion is:

\begin{equation}
	l/d < \theta \label{eq:3.29}
\end{equation}


where $l$ is the side length of the cubic cell, $d$ is the distance from the target particle to the node's center of mass, and $\theta$ is an accuracy parameter. If this condition is met, the entire mass of the node is treated as a single point mass at its center of mass, providing an acceptable approximation when the node is sufficiently distant (i.e., appears small in angular size). The recursion stops upon reaching a leaf node, which by definition represents a single particle and is interacted with directly. This approximation reduces the computational cost of the force calculation from $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(N \log{N})$ and adapts efficiently to clustered mass distributions, making it suitable for collisionless systems \citep{barnes1986hierarchical}.

The standard geometric criterion (Equation~\ref{eq:3.29}), however, can fail in some circumstances \citep{salmon1994skeletons}. Therefore, \texttt{Fi} implements a modified criterion following \citet{springel2002cosmological}:

\begin{equation}
	M_n l^4 < \alpha |\mathbf{a}| d^6
\end{equation}

where $M_n$ is the total mass contained in the node, $\mathbf{a}$ is an estimate of the gravitational acceleration (typically from the previous timestep), and $\alpha$ is a parameter to control accuracy.

\subsection{Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics}
\label{subsec:3.3.2_SPH}

The gas dynamics are solved using the Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics (SPH) method \citep{gingold1977smoothed, lucy1977numerical}. In SPH, the hydrodynamic equations are solved from a Lagrangian perspective, representing the fluid with a discrete set of particles rather than a continuous medium. For an inviscid fluid described by density $\rho$, pressure $p$, and specific internal energy $u$, the Lagrangian takes the form:

\begin{equation}
	L = \int \left[ \frac{1}{2} v^2 - u(\rho) \right] \rho d^3\mathbf{x},
	\label{eq:3_Lag_clasic}
\end{equation}
where the specific internal energy satisfies $\partial u / \partial \rho = p/\rho^2$, and $\mathbf{r} = (x_1, x_2, x_3)$ is the position vector. From this Lagrangian, the Euler equations of motion can be derived via a variational principle \citep{rasio2000particle}:

\begin{align}
	\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{v}) &= 0 \label{eq:3_32} \\
	\frac{\partial \mathbf{v}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla) \mathbf{v} &= -\frac{\nabla p}{\rho} \label{eq:3_33} \\
	\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla) u &= -\frac{p}{\rho} (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}) \label{eq:3_34}
\end{align}

Equations \eqref{eq:3_32}, \eqref{eq:3_33}, and \eqref{eq:3_34} are the continuity, momentum, and energy equations, respectively. 

To obtain a discrete representation, the continuous Lagrangian is replaced by a particle-based analogue:

\begin{equation}
	L_{\mathrm{SPH}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} m_i \left[ \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{v}_i^2 - u_i \right],
	\label{eq:3_lag_discrete}
\end{equation}
where $m_i$, $\mathbf{v}_i$, and $\rho_i$ are the mass, velocity, and density of each particle (representing a fluid element), and $u_i = u(\rho_i)$ is the specific internal energy. To use Eq.~\eqref{eq:3_lag_discrete} as the system's Lagrangian, a suitable expression for the density in terms of the particle coordinates is required.

A key ingredient of the method is the density estimate. In SPH, the density at any point $\mathbf{r}$ is estimated by interpolating over neighboring particles:

\begin{equation}
	\rho(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{j} m_j W(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_j; h),
	\label{eq:3_dens_SPH}
\end{equation}

where $W(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_j; h)$ is a smoothing kernel of characteristic width $h$ (the smoothing length). The kernel must satisfy two conditions: its integral over all space must be unity, and it should approach a Dirac delta function in the limit $h \to 0$. The first condition ensures that integrating Eq.~\eqref{eq:3_dens_SPH} over space recovers the total mass of the system.


In the case of \texttt{Fi}, the cubic spline kernel is adopted \citep{pelupessy2005numerical,monaghan1992smoothed}:

\begin{equation}
	W(r,h) = \frac{1}{\pi h^3}
	\begin{cases}
		1 - \frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{r}{h}\right)^2 + \frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{r}{h}\right)^3, & 0 \leq \frac{r}{h} \leq 1, \\[2ex]
		\frac{1}{4}\left(2 - \frac{r}{h}\right)^3, & 1 \leq \frac{r}{h} \leq 2, \\[2ex]
		0, & \text{otherwise}.
	\end{cases}
	\label{eq:monaghan_lattanzio_kernel}
\end{equation}

With the density estimation and kernel defined, we now turn to the treatment of the smoothing length $h$. In modern SPH implementations, it is common to use an adaptive smoothing length $h_i$ for each particle, adjusted to keep the number of neighboring particles approximately constant. However, the use of adaptive smoothing lengths introduces $\nabla h$ terms in the equations, which can lead to violations of energy and entropy conservation if not properly accounted for \citep{nelson1994variable}. A rigorous way to incorporate variable smoothing lengths is to treat $h_i$ as additional variables in the Lagrangian formulation, subject to constraints \citep{springel2002cosmological}. In \texttt{Fi}, the following constraint is imposed:

\begin{equation}
	\frac{4\pi}{3} h_i^3 (\rho_i + \tilde{\rho}) = M_{\mathrm{SPH}},
	\label{eq:3_sph_h_constraint}
\end{equation}

where $M_{\mathrm{SPH}} = \bar{m} N_{\mathrm{nb}}$, with $\bar{m}$ the mean particle mass and $N_{\mathrm{nb}}$ the target number of neighbors. The term $\tilde{\rho}$ is an extra density introduced to prevent $h_i$ from diverging as $\rho_i \to 0$ \citep{pelupessy2005numerical}. This constraint can be incorporated into the variational principle via Lagrangian multipliers, yielding correction factors in the equations of motion (see Appendix~\ref{ap:SPH_equations}).

With the notation $W_{ij}(h_i) \equiv W(|\mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j|, h_i)$ and the symmetrized kernel $\bar{W}_{ij} = [W_{ij}(h_i) + W_{ij}(h_j)]/2$, the equation of motion for particle $i$ becomes:

\begin{equation}
	\frac{d\mathbf{v}_i}{dt} = -\sum_{j=1}^N m_j \left[
	f_i \frac{P_i}{\rho_i^2} \nabla_i W_{ij}(h_i) +
	f_j \frac{P_j}{\rho_j^2} \nabla_i W_{ij}(h_j) +
	\Pi_{ij} \nabla_i \bar{W}_{ij}
	\right],
	\label{eq:3.42}
\end{equation}

where the correction factor $f_i$ accounts for the variability of the smoothing length:

\begin{equation}
	f_i = \left( 1 + \frac{h_i}{3(\rho_i + \tilde{\rho})} \frac{\partial \rho_i}{\partial h_i} \right)^{-1}.
	\label{eq:sph_fi_factor}
\end{equation}


The first two terms come from a conservative derivation of SPH equations.To handle discontinuities such as shocks, an artificial viscosity (AV) term $\Pi_{ij}$ is included. In SPH, entropy generation is confined to shocks through this viscous term, which also prevents unphysical particle interpenetration. 

\texttt{Fi} implements the standard Monaghan artificial viscosity \citep{monaghan1992smoothed, pelupessy2005numerical}:

\begin{equation}
	\Pi_{ij} = \frac{-\alpha \mu_{ij} \bar{c}_{ij} + \beta \mu_{ij}^2}{\bar{\rho}_{ij}}
	\label{eq:3.AV}
\end{equation}

with

\begin{equation}
	\mu_{ij} = 
	\begin{cases}
		\frac{\mathbf{v}_{ij} \cdot \mathbf{r}_{ij}}{\bar{h}_{ij}(\mathbf{r}_{ij}^2/\bar{h}_{ij}^2 + \eta^2)}, & \text{for~} \mathbf{v}_{ij} \cdot \mathbf{r}_{ij} < 0, \\
		0, & \text{for~} \mathbf{v}_{ij} \cdot \mathbf{r}_{ij} \geq 0
	\end{cases}
	\label{eq:3.45}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{v}_{ij} = \mathbf{v}_i - \mathbf{v}_j$ and $\mathbf{r}_{ij} = \mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j$. The dimensionless parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$ control the strength of the viscosity (default values are $\alpha = 0.5$, $\beta = 1.0$), and $\eta \ll 1$ prevents numerical divergences. Quantities overlaid with a bar are symmetrized averages, e.g., $\bar{c}_{ij} = (c_i + c_j)/2$. 

The AV is only activated when particles are approaching each other ($\mathbf{v}_{ij} \cdot \mathbf{r}_{ij} < 0$), ensuring that $\mu_{ij} \leq 0$ and therefore $\Pi_{ij} \geq 0$. As the term $-\nabla_i W_{ij}(h_i)$ is proportional to $(\mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j) = \mathbf{r}_{ij}$, the AV acts to decrease kinetic energy, with the dissipated energy converted into thermal energy, thereby increasing the internal energy (or, equivalently, the entropic function, depending on the thermodynamic formulation chosen) of the particles.

Finally, the thermodynamic evolution of the gas must be specified. \texttt{Fi} offers two formulations: evolving either the specific internal energy $u_i$ or the entropic function $A_i$ (related to the specific entropy). For an ideal gas with adiabatic index $\gamma$, these quantities are related through

\begin{align}
	P_i &= A_i \rho_i^{\gamma}, \label{eq:3.43_poly_eq_state}\\
	u_i &= \frac{A_i}{\gamma - 1} \rho_i^{\gamma - 1} = \frac{P_i}{(\gamma - 1)\rho_i} \label{eq:3.44_int_energy},
\end{align}

where $A_i$ is the entropic function, which remains constant for adiabatic processes in the absence of shocks. The evolution equation for the entropic function is

\begin{equation}
	\frac{dA_i}{dt} = -\frac{\gamma-1}{\rho_i^\gamma} (\Gamma - \Lambda) + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\gamma-1}{\rho_i^{\gamma-1}} \sum_{j=1}^N m_j \Pi_{ij} \mathbf{v}_{ij} \cdot \nabla_i \bar{W}_{ij}
\end{equation}

where $\Gamma$ and $\Lambda$ represent external heating and cooling rates (both set to zero in our simulations). Alternatively, the internal energy equation can be integrated:

\begin{equation}
	\frac{d u_i}{d t} = \sum_{j=1}^N m_j \left[ \frac{f_i}{2} \frac{p_i}{\rho_i^2} \nabla_i W_{ij}(h_i) +  \frac{f_j}{2} \frac{p_j}{\rho_j^2} \nabla_i W_{ij}(h_j)  + \Pi_{ij} \nabla_i \bar{W}_{ij}\right] \mathbf{v}_{ij} \label{eq:sph_energy_eq}
\end{equation}

For our simulations, we adopt the energy equation (Eq.~\eqref{eq:sph_energy_eq}), as it provides better numerical stability in the coupled stellar-gas framework (see Appendix~\ref{ap:SPH_equations}).

\section{Coupling Strategy: The BRIDGE Method}
\label{sec:3.4_Coupling_codes}

The N-body and SPH codes operate as independent modules and must be coupled for mixed star-gas simulations. A key advantage of using SPH for the gas component is that both systems are represented by particles, which simplifies the implementation of gravitational interactions. In this section, we describe the coupling scheme employed in this work, based on the \texttt{BRIDGE} method \citep{fujii2007bridge} and following the implementation of \citet{reinoso2024formation}.

\subsection{Symplectic Integrator}
\label{subsec:3.4.1_Symplectic_integrator}

The \texttt{BRIDGE} method \citep{fujii2007bridge} employs a symplectic integrator, specifically the second-order leapfrog scheme. The evolution of a dynamical system can be described by Hamilton's equations:

\begin{equation}
	\frac{df}{dt} = \{f, H\},
\end{equation}

where ${f, H}$ denotes the Poisson bracket:

\begin{equation}
	\{f, H\} = \sum_{i} \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i} \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}  - \frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i} \frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}\right) = D_H f(t)
\end{equation}

with $(q_i, p_i)$ being the canonical coordinates. The formal solution of this equation is given by the exponential operator:

\begin{equation}
	f(t) = e^{tD_H}f(0)
\end{equation}

For a gravitational Hamiltonian of the form

\begin{equation}
	H(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{p}) = \frac{\mathbf{p}^2}{2m} + \Phi(\mathbf{x}) \equiv H_{\mathrm{kin}}(\mathbf{p}) + H_{\mathrm{pot}}(\mathbf{x}),
\end{equation}

the Liouville operator $D_H$ can be decomposed as:

\begin{align} 
	D_H &= \frac{\partial H}{\partial \mathbf{p}} \cdot \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf{x}} - \frac{\partial H}{\partial \mathbf{x}} \cdot \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf{p}} \\ 
	&= \frac{\mathbf{p}}{m} \cdot \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf{x}} + (-\nabla \Phi(\mathbf{x})) \cdot \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf{p}} \\ 
	&\equiv D_{\mathrm{drift}} + D_{\mathrm{kick}}. 
\end{align}

The solution after a time step $\Delta t$ is then:

\begin{equation}
	f(t+\delta t) = e^{\delta t D_H}f(t) \label{eq:3.51}
\end{equation}

When applied to the phase-space coordinates $(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{v})$, the exponential operator $e^{\delta t D_H}$ has no closed-form expression due to the coupling between position and velocity \citep{zwart2018astrophysical}. However, the individual operators $D_{\mathrm{drift}}$ and $D_{\mathrm{kick}}$ have simple actions:

\begin{align} 
	D_{\mathrm{drift}}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{v}) &= (\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{0}), \\ 
	D_{\mathrm{kick}}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{v}) &= (\mathbf{0}, -\nabla \Phi(\mathbf{x})). 
\end{align}

The drift operator advances positions at constant velocity, while the kick operator updates velocities at constant position. Importantly, these operators do not commute: $[D_{\mathrm{drift}}, D_{\mathrm{kick}}] \neq 0$. However, the exponential operator can be approximated using an operator splitting technique \citep{fujii2007bridge}:

\begin{equation} 
	e^{\Delta t D_H} = e^{\delta t (D_{\mathrm{kick}} + D_{\mathrm{drift}})} = \prod_{i=1}^{k} e^{a_i \delta t D_{\mathrm{kick}}} e^{b_i \delta t D_{\mathrm{drift}}} + \mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{k+1}). 
\end{equation}

For $k=2$ with coefficients $a_1 = a_2 = 1/2$, $b_1 = 1$, $b_2 = 0$, we obtain the second-order accurate approximation:

\begin{equation} 
	e^{\delta t (D_{\mathrm{kick}} + D_{\mathrm{drift}})} \approx e^{(\delta t/2) D_{\mathrm{kick}}} e^{\delta t D_{\mathrm{drift}}} e^{(\delta t/2) D_{\mathrm{kick}}} + \mathcal{O}(\delta t^3).
\end{equation}


This leads to the kick-drift-kick leapfrog integrator, which proceeds as follows for a time step $\Delta t$:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item Kick: Advance velocities by $\Delta t/2$ using accelerations computed at the current positions:
	\[
	\mathbf{v}_0 \to \mathbf{v}_{1/2} = \mathbf{v}_0 + \frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbf{a}_0
	\]
	\item Drift: Advance positions by $\Delta t$ using the new half-step velocities:
	\[
	\mathbf{x}_0 \to \mathbf{x}_1 = \mathbf{x}_0 + \Delta t \mathbf{v}_{1/2}
	\]
	\item Kick: Advance velocities by another $\Delta t/2$ using accelerations computed at the updated positions:
	\[
	\mathbf{v}_{1/2} \to \mathbf{v}_1 = \mathbf{v}_{1/2} + \frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbf{a}_1
	\]
\end{enumerate}

where $\mathbf{a}_0 = \mathbf{a}(\mathbf{x}_0)$ and $\mathbf{a}_1 = \mathbf{a}(\mathbf{x}_1)$ are the accelerations due to the gravitational potential. The subscripts $0$, $1/2$, and $1$ denote quantities evaluated at times $t$, $t + \Delta t/2$, and $t + \Delta t$, respectively.

\subsection{BRIDGE}
\label{sec:3.4.2_Bridge}

AMUSE includes an implementation of the original \texttt{BRIDGE} scheme. An example coupling an N-body code with an SPH code can be found in the AMUSE distribution at:

\path{amuse/examples/simple/gas_in_cluster.py}

This example models a star cluster with an embedded gas component, coupling GADGET-2 (SPH) and HERMITE (N-body). In this implementation, each code performs its own drift step internally. The kick step, however, is handled externally as follows:

\begin{itemize}
	\item Accelerations $\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{NB},i}$ for each N-body particle due to all SPH particles are computed using the SPH code's \pythonfunc{get\_gravity\_at\_point}(\pythonarg{eps}, \pythonarg{$x$}, \pythonarg{$y$}, \pythonarg{$z$}) method, which returns the gravitational acceleration at a given point $(x,y,z)$ with softening length \pythonarg{eps}. The velocities of N-body particles are then kicked using these accelerations.
	\item Similarly, accelerations $\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{SPH},i}$ for each SPH particle due to all N-body particles are computed using the \pythonfunc{get\_gravity\_at\_point}(\pythonarg{eps}, \pythonarg{$x$}, \pythonarg{$y$}, \pythonarg{$z$}) method of a tree code (BHTree in the example), with the softening length set to the SPH smoothing length $h_i$. The velocities of SPH particles are then kicked accordingly.
\end{itemize}

As identified by \citet{reinoso2024formation}, this implementation has two main issues, both related to the kick steps. First, SPH and N-body particles use different gravitational softening lengths ($h$ and $\epsilon$, respectively), leading to asymmetric forces:

\begin{equation} 
	F_{\mathrm{NB}} = \frac{G m_{\mathrm{NB}} m_{\mathrm{SPH}}}{(r^2 + \epsilon^2)^{3/2}} r \neq F_{\mathrm{SPH}} = \frac{G m_{\mathrm{NB}} m_{\mathrm{SPH}}}{(r^2 + h^2)^{3/2}} r, 
\end{equation}

where $r = |\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{NB}} - \mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{SPH}}|$. This asymmetry can be resolved by introducing an effective softening length $\bar{\epsilon}$ for the mutual gravitational interaction during the kick steps.

Second, the force calculation may use different softening kernels: SPH codes often employ a cubic spline softening, while N-body codes typically use a Plummer (or similar) softening. The difference between these softening formulations is illustrated in Figure~\ref{img:softening_force_comparison}.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Plots/force_comparison_1.png}
	\caption{Comparison of force calculation normalized (masses and gravitational constant set to 1). In dashed green, the exact gravitational force. In blue, the force with plummer softening, and in red, the force calculated with cubic spline softening.}
	\label{img:softening_force_comparison}
\end{figure}

To handle these issues, \citet{reinoso2024formation} developed a modified \texttt{BRIDGE} implementation that uses the \texttt{FASTKICK} code to compute gravitational interactions between SPH and N-body particles. \texttt{FASTKICK} computes forces using a Plummer softening with a constant softening length $\bar{\epsilon}$, ensuring symmetry and consistency. The code provides a Python interface, making it compatible with AMUSE simulation scripts, with the computationally intensive force calculations performed in compiled languages (C/Fortran), and a GPU-accelerated version (C++/CUDA) is also available.

The modified \texttt{BRIDGE} algorithm proceeds as follows for each global time step $\Delta t$:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item At time $t$, compute accelerations $\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{NB},i}$ for all N-body particles due to SPH particles, and $\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{SPH},i}$ for all SPH particles due to N-body particles, using \texttt{FASTKICK} with softening length $\bar{\epsilon}$. Kick velocities by $\Delta t/2$.
	\item Evolve each code independently for a full time step $\Delta t$ (drift), updating positions via their own internal integrators.
	\item Recompute accelerations using \texttt{FASTKICK} with the updated positions, and apply a second velocity kick of $\Delta t/2$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Adaptive timestep}
\label{sec:3.4.3_Adaptive_tstep}

The evolution of the coupled system is controlled by the \texttt{BRIDGE} time step. At the end of each \texttt{BRIDGE} step, the time step is adjusted to accurately resolve the physics of the gas and the star-gas interaction. For the gas, the minimum free-fall time is calculated:

\begin{equation}
	t_{\rm ff} = \sqrt{\frac{3 \pi}{32 G \rho_{\max}}}~,
\end{equation}

where $\rho_{\max}$ is the maximum density among all SPH particles. For the gas-stars interaction, Courant-type time steps that depend on the acceleration between gas and star particles are calculated:

\begin{align}
	\Delta t_{\rm coup, gas} =& \min_i \left(f_{\rm coup} \sqrt{\frac{2h_i}{\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{SPH}, i}}}\right) \label{eq:delta_t_coup:gas} \\
	\Delta t_{\rm coup, stars} =& \min_i \left(f_{\rm coup} \sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{\rm coup}}{\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{NB, i}}}}\right)~,
	\label{eq:delta_t_coup:stars}
\end{align}

where $h_i$ is the softening length of SPH particles, $\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{SPH}, i}$ and $\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{NB, i}}$ are the forces calculated by \texttt{FASTKICK} in the kick step, and $\epsilon_{\rm coup}$ is the softening length used by \texttt{FASTKICK} in the calculation of gravitational interactions. For $f_{\rm coup}=1$, Equations~\eqref{eq:delta_t_coup:gas} and~\eqref{eq:delta_t_coup:stars} set the minimum resolution for gas and stars interactions to be solved in the \texttt{BRIDGE} steps. Since $f_{\rm coup}$ is a security parameter, it is recommended to use values $<1$.

Taking into account the minimum gas free-fall time (Equation~\eqref{eq:delta_t_coup:stars}) and the Courant-type time steps (Equations~\eqref{eq:delta_t_coup:gas} and \eqref{eq:delta_t_coup:stars}), the relevant time scale yields

\begin{equation}
	\tau_{\rm step} = \min(t_{\rm ff}, \Delta t_{\rm coup, gas}, \Delta t_{\rm coup, stars})~.
\end{equation}

Finally, the time step of \texttt{BRIDGE} is adjusted by the condition

\begin{equation}
	0.5\tau_{\rm step} \leq \frac{\tau}{2^n} \leq \tau_{\rm step}~,
	\label{eq:Bridge_tstep_cond}
\end{equation}

with $\tau$ the crossing time of the stellar cluster and $n$ an integer. The time step of the \texttt{BRIDGE} $\Delta t_{\rm BRIDGE} = \tau/2^n$ is adjusted at the end of each \texttt{BRIDGE} step, and is also used in SPH code. Note that the adjusted time step is a power of two of the initial N-body time unit, consistent with the block time steps used for the N-body particles. Consequently, the three interacting codes are in synchrony. Figure~\ref{img:Bridge_diagram} shows a schematic of the coupling strategy.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Esquemas/Bridge_Diagram}
	\caption{Scheme of the \texttt{BRIDGE} coupling strategy. In the kick steps, \texttt{FASTKICK} computes the mutual gravitational forces between stars and gas, and the velocities of both components are updated. In the drift step, each code evolves its particles independently: \texttt{Ph4} handles stellar dynamics (including collision detection and mergers), while \texttt{Fi} evolves the gas. After the kick-drift-kick cycle, the global \texttt{BRIDGE} time step is updated based on the gas free-fall time and the coupling timescales ($t_{\rm ff}$, $\Delta t_{\rm coup, gas}$, $\Delta t_{\rm coup, stars}$). This updated timestep is used for both the next \texttt{BRIDGE} step and the \texttt{Fi} integration. Additionally, escaped N-body particles are identified and tagged in the post-processing phase.}
	\label{img:Bridge_diagram}

\section{Plummer distribution}
\label{sec:4.1_Plummer_dist}

The scenario presented in Chapter \ref{chap:2_SMBH_formation_scenario} and proposed by \citet{escala2021observational} was previously explored by \citet{vergara2023global} through simulations sampling different collisional regimes. These models represent stellar systems characterized by their initial mass and size scale. The stellar clusters are initialized using a Plummer distribution \citep{plummer1911problem}. The Plummer model is characterized by the potential \citep{2008gady.book.....B}:

\begin{equation}
	\Phi_p(r) = -\frac{GM}{\sqrt{r^2 + b^2}},\quad \text{with } r = |\mathbf{r}|
\end{equation}

where $b$ is the Plummer scale. From the Poisson equation, the associated density is

\begin{equation}
	\rho_p(r) = \frac{3M}{4\pi b^3} \left(1+\frac{r^2}{b^2}\right)^{-5/2}
\end{equation}

The potential energy of a Plummer model is:

\begin{equation}
	U_p = - \frac{3 \pi G M^2}{32b}
	\label{eq:4.3_Upl}
\end{equation}

We now require a description for the velocity. The velocity dispersion $\sigma$ for an isotropic spherical stellar system can be obtained through the Jeans equation \citep{2008gady.book.....B}:

\begin{equation}
	\frac{d(\rho \sigma^2)}{d r} = -\rho \frac{d \Phi}{d r}
\end{equation}

Solving for the Plummer model gives the velocity dispersion:

\begin{equation}
	\sigma_p(r)^2 = \frac{GM}{6\sqrt{r^2 + b^2}}
\end{equation}

We can relate the Plummer scale parameter $b$ to a physical scale. First, we use the definition of virial radius as the characteristic system radius $R$ via the potential energy $U$:

\begin{align}
	-\frac{GM^2}{2R} =& U \\
	\frac{1}{R} =& -\frac{2}{GM^2}U~,
	\label{eq:4.7_virial_radius}
\end{align}

where $U$ is the total gravitational potential energy. In the case of a system of $N$ particles, this radius is defined as:

\begin{equation}
	R = \left(\frac{1}{M^2} \sum_{i,j \neq i}^{N}\frac{m_i m_j}{|\mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r_j}|}\right)^{-1}
\end{equation}

In the case of a Plummer model, replacing Eq.~\ref{eq:4.3_Upl} into Eq.~\ref{eq:4.7_virial_radius}, this radius can be expressed in function of the Plummer scale parameter $b$:

\begin{align}
	\frac{1}{R} =& \left(-\frac{2}{GM^2}\right) \left(-\frac{3\pi GM^2}{32b}\right) \\
	R =& \frac{16b}{3\pi}
\end{align}

Once the physical scale of the system is defined via $b$ (or equivalently $R$), we need to generate the actual particle distribution. This is typically done in a dimensionless system of units, which we now describe.

\subsection{N-body units}
\label{sec:4.2_Nbody_units}

Most gravitational N-body codes employ a dimensionless system of units, known as N-body units or Hénon units (see \citet{10.1007/BFb0116419}). In this system, physical quantities are scaled to be of order unity, which brings two main benefits: (i) it eliminates the need to multiply by $G$ in every force calculation, and (ii) it improves numerical precision by keeping variables close to unity, thereby minimizing round-off errors that would otherwise arise when working with astrophysical scales spanning many orders of magnitude. Additionally, N-body units facilitate comparison between results obtained with different codes.

N-body units are defined by setting the gravitational constant, total mass, and total energy to convenient values:

\begin{align}
	G =& 1 \\
	M =& 1 \\
	E =& -\frac{1}{4}
\end{align}

With this, the units of mass, length, and time can be defined. The mass unit is simply the total mass:

\begin{equation}
	u_m = M
\end{equation}

Assuming virial equilibrium ($2T + U = 0$), the kinetic and potential energies become:

\begin{align}
	T =& \frac{1}{4} = - E \\
	U =& -\frac{1}{2} = 2E \label{eq:4.17_pot_en}
\end{align}

Using the virial radius definition (Eq.~\ref{eq:4.7_virial_radius})and substituting the potential energy from Eq.~\ref{eq:4.17_pot_en}, we obtain:

\begin{equation}
	R = -\frac{GM^2}{4E}
\end{equation}

Using $G=1$, $M=1$ and $E=-1/4$, we find that $R=1$, so the length unit $u_l$ is:

\begin{equation}
	u_l = R = -\frac{GM^2}{4E}
\end{equation}

Finally, we derive the time unit. A characteristic velocity follows from the kinetic energy:

\begin{align}
	T =& \frac{M \sigma^2}{2} \\
	\sigma^2 =& \frac{2T}{M} = \frac{-2E}{M}
\end{align}

The crossing time is defined as:

\begin{equation}
	t_{cross} = \frac{2R}{\sigma} = \frac{GM^{5/2}}{(-2E)^{3/2}}
\end{equation}

With $G=1$, $M=1$, and $E=-1/4$, we obtain $t_{\mathrm{cross}} = 2^{3/2}$. The standard N-body time unit is then defined as:

\begin{equation}
	u_t = t_{cross}2^{-2/3} = \frac{GM^{5/2}}{(-4E)^{3/2}}
\end{equation}

which indeed yields $u_t = 1$ for $G=1$, $M=1$ and $E=-1/4$. Note that defining the unit system requires only a scale radius and the total mass of the system. AMUSE provides its own physical units system, which can be converted to N-body units using a converter. In AMUSE, N-body units are handled through the \pythonfunc{nbody\_system} class, which requires at least two physical quantities, typically the total mass and the virial radius. The following example illustrates how to initialize a converter:

\begin{lstlisting}[caption=How to define a converter in AMUSE, label=lst:create_converter]
	from amuse.units import units, nbody_system
	
	R_vir = 1 | units.pc          # Define a scale radius
	M_tot = 1e4 | units.MSun  # Define the total mass
	
	converter = nbody_system.nbody_to_si(R_vir, M_tot)
\end{lstlisting}

The converter serves a bidirectional role: it translates physical quantities (e.g., positions, velocities, masses) into dimensionless N-body units for the numerical calculations, and converts the simulation output back to dimensional physical units for subsequent analysis.

\subsection{Particles generation}

Both the stellar and gaseous systems are generated using the AMUSE implementation of the Plummer model. To generate a stellar system with the initial conditions of model A, for example, we first initialize a converter:

\begin{lstlisting}[caption=Converter example for model A, label=lst:conv_model_A]
	from amuse.units import units, nbody_system
	
	M_stars = 5e4 | units.MSun
	R_vir = 0.005 | units.pc
	converter = nbody_system.nbody_to_si(R_vir, M_stars)
\end{lstlisting}

After importing the \pythonfunc{plummer} module, the stellar particles are generated by calling the function \pythonfunc{new\_plummer\_model} with the converter and number of particles:

\begin{lstlisting}[caption=Stars particles in model A, label=lst:stars_part_A]
	from amuse.ic import plummer
	
	N = 1000
	stars = plummer.new_plummer_model(N, converter, radius_cutoff=5., do_scale=True)
	stars.radius = 11.7 | units.RSun
\end{lstlisting}

The \pythonfunc{radius\_cutoff} parameter truncates the distribution at $5b$, where $b$ is the Plummer scale parameter (here corresponding to a virial radius $R_v = 0.005$ pc). Setting \pythonfunc{do\_scale=True} ensures that the generated particles are scaled to N-body units, satisfying the virial conditions described in Section \ref{sec:4.2_Nbody_units} (total mass $M=1$, total energy $E=-1/4$, kinetic energy $T=1/4$, and potential energy $U=-1/2$).

For the gaseous component, we use the \pythonfunc{gasplummer} function from the \pythonfunc{amuse.ic} module to generate the SPH particles. The number of gas particles is chosen such that the mass ratio $m_{\rm SPH} / m_{\rm NB} = 0.01$, where $m_{\rm NB}$ is the mass of each N-body particle. For example, for the variation with $20\%$, A$_{20}$, the gas particles are generated as follows:

\begin{lstlisting}[caption=Gas particles in model A, label=lst:gas_part_A]
	from amuse.ic import gasplummer
	
	f_gas = 0.2
	N_gas = N*100
	N_gas = int(N_gas*f_gas)
	
	gas = gasplummer.new_plummer_gas_model(N_gas, 
	                                                             convert_nbody=converter, 
	                                                             do_scale=True, 
	                                                             mass_cutoff=0.95535)
	gas.mass = (M_stars*f_gas / N_gas)
\end{lstlisting}

While the stellar component is initialized in virial equilibrium with respect to its own mass, the gas particles are initially at rest. To prevent immediate collapse, their internal energy is set to achieve hydrostatic equilibrium in the gravitational potential of the stellar cluster. For a fluid in hydrostatic equilibrium,

\begin{equation}
	\nabla p = - \rho \nabla \Phi~,
\end{equation}

where $p$, $\rho$, and $\Phi$ stand for the pressure, density, and gravitational potential, respectively. Assuming a polytropic equation of state (Eqs.~\ref{eq:3.43_poly_eq_state} and \ref{eq:3.44_int_energy}), this condition gives a relation for the specific internal energy $u$:

\begin{equation}
	u = - \frac{\Phi}{\gamma}~,
\end{equation}

where $\gamma$ is the polytropic index. 

The initialization described in Listing~\ref{lst:gas_part_A} raises a potential issue. The gas particles are generated using a converter scaled to the total stellar mass ($M = 5\times10^4\,\mathrm{M_\odot}$), and their internal energy is set accordingly to achieve hydrostatic equilibrium in the combined potential. However, after generation, we explicitly reduce the gas mass to $f_{\mathrm{gas}} M$ (in this case, $20\%$ of the stellar mass). In isolation, this reduction would lead to an imbalance: the gravitational force from the gas diminishes, while the internal pressure remains unchanged, causing the gaseous component to expand. However, because the gas is embedded in and gravitationally coupled to the stellar system, the dominant contribution to the potential comes from the stars. We therefore expect the gas to remain near dynamical equilibrium, with any minor adjustments occurring on a dynamical timescale as the simulation evolves.

\section{Numerical Parameters}
\label{sec:4.3_Numerical_parameters}

Having described the numerical methods in the previous chapter, we now detail the specific parameter configurations used for each code. As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:3.1_AMUSE}, the AMUSE framework provides a unified interface that connects Python scripts with compiled codes through class structures. Each code is accessible via the community module, e.g., \pythonfunc{amuse.community.ph4.interface} for \texttt{Ph4}. The following example illustrates how to create an instance of \texttt{Ph4}:

\begin{lstlisting}[caption=Instance creation for \texttt{Ph4}, label=lst:Ph4_instance]
	from amuse.community.ph4.interface import ph4
	
	grav = ph4()
\end{lstlisting}

In listing~\ref{lst:Ph4_instance}, \pythonfunc{grav} is an instance of the class \pythonfunc{amuse.community.ph4.interface.ph4} (shortened \pythonfunc{ph4}). The code classes have a variety of parameters and internal methods, whose principal functionality is to set up the parameters and particles before running the code. Most codes run in N-body units; however, they have the possibility to work with physical units. In the following, we show how to correctly initialize and run an instance of \pythonfunc{ph4}, including particle creation using a Plummer distribution and a converter:

\begin{lstlisting}[caption=Example setup for \texttt{Ph4} with particles, label=lst:Ph4_inst_and_run]
	from amuse.community.ph4.interface import ph4
	from amuse.units import units, nbody_system
	from amuse.ic.plummer import new_plummer_model
	
	# Step 1, creation of a converter and particles
	virial_radius = 1 | units.pc
	total_mass = 1000 | units.MSun
	N_stars = 1000
	
	converter = nbody_system.nbody_to_si(total_mass, virial_radius)
	
	bodies = new_plummer_model(N_stars, 
	                                           convert_nbody=converter, 
	                                           do_scale=True)
	bodies.radius = 1 | units.RSun
	
	# Step 2, creation of an instance of the code
	grav = ph4(converter, 
						 mode = 'cpu',
						 number_of_workers=1)
	
	grav.initialize_code()
	grav.parameters.epsilon_squared = (1 | units.RSun) ** 2
	
	# Step 3, adding particles
	grav.particles.add_particles(bodies)
	grav.commit_particles()
	
	# Step 4, enable collision detection
	sc_col_det = grav.stopping_conditions.collision_detection
	sc_col_det.enable()
\end{lstlisting}

In listing~\ref{lst:Ph4_inst_and_run}, we have created a converter. The principal function of the converter is to translate units from the N-body system of units (see Section~\ref{sec:4.2_Nbody_units}) to the International System of Units (SI). The converter needs the virial radius and total mass, then escalates quantities such that one N-body unit of length is equal to the virial radius. When the instance of \pythonfunc{ph4} is created, we provide the converter, the \pythonfunc{mode} (where options are \pythonfunc{'gpu'} and \pythonfunc{'cpu'}), and the \pythonfunc{number\_of\_workers}, that controls the number of CPUs used in parallel calculations. Since the stopping condition for detection of collisions is enabled, it is necessary to provide a function to handle collisions. We can then evolve the instance of \pythonfunc{grav}. The next listing shows a minimal evolution loop:

\begin{lstlisting}[caption=Loop with evolution for \texttt{Ph4}, label=lst:Ph4_loop_evolve]
	from amuse.community.ph4.interface import ph4
	from amuse.units import units, nbody_system
	from amuse.ic.plummer import new_plummer_model
	
	t_end = 1000 | units.yr
	
	# Principal loop
	while grav.model_time < t_end:
		grav.evolve_model(t_end)
		
		if sc_col_det.is_set():
			solve_collision(grav, sc_col_det)
\end{lstlisting}

where \pythonfunc{solve\_collision} is a function that handles the collisions. Note that when a collision is detected, the code stops before reaching the final time. Then, if there is no function to correctly solve the collisions, the loop could be infinite. Since the structures provided by AMUSE are generic, the initialization of most codes is very similar.

\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{sec:cap.6_conclusions}

In this thesis, we have studied the role of gas in the runaway collision process that forms SMBH seeds, within the scenario proposed by \citet{escala2021observational}. We performed 48 simulations based on the initial conditions of \citet{vergara2023global}, with the inclusion of a gaseous component. Our simulations couple N-body and SPH codes via the \texttt{BRIDGE} scheme. Our main findings are summarized as follows (see Chapter~\ref{cap:5_results}):

\begin{enumerate}
	\item The core collapse time is shortened by the presence of gas. For gas fractions of 5\%, 10\%, and 20\% the core collapse time is reduced by 6.23\%, 15.52\%, and 27.39\% on average, respectively.
	\item The BH formation efficiency is enhanced by gas. In our simulations, we find that the average increase in BH mass is $8.40 \pm 15.89$\%, $22.51 \pm 20.57$\%, and $44.14 \pm 21.32$\% for gas fractions of 5\%, 10\%, and 20\%, respectively. The BH formation efficiency follows the same trend, with increases of $12.56 \pm 16.27$\%, $27.01 \pm 20.79$\%, and $45.10 \pm 31.39$\% for the same gas fractions. The enhancement is more pronounced in the models with the highest collision rates within the IR (model families A, B, C, H).
	\item The fitted model for BH formation efficiency shows that the effect of gas is most important in the intermediate range $10^{-4}\leq M/M_{\rm crit}\leq 10^5$, where $\epsilon_{\rm BH}$ increases with both the gas fraction and $M/M_{\rm crit}$, and exhibits a smoother slope than the model of \citet{vergara2023global}.
	\item Since gas dynamical friction is not fully resolved in our simulations, the observed effects are a consequence of gas-star gravitational interactions, then our results may be conservative.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Caveats}
\label{sec:caveats}

While our simulations demonstrate the potential of gas to enhance SMBH seed formation, several simplifications remain to be addressed. The gas is simulated using an adiabatic equation of state, evolving the energy equation instead of the entropic function \citep{pelupessy2005numerical}. Radiative cooling, heating, and stellar feedback are not included. Supernovae are also not included; however, since our simulations run for only $5~{\rm Myr}$, the results remain valid within this timescale. Gas accretion is not included, and dynamical friction is not fully resolved; consequently, the effects observed may be enhanced in a more realistic scenario. The N-body code used does not include regularization for close encounters, so collisions are resolved by force, which slows the evolution. Due to the limitations on simulating a large number of particles, our simulations form IMBHs rather than SMBHs.

Our findings address the question of how gas affects the global instability that leads to runaway collisions and, eventually, to the formation of a SMBH. The next natural question is whether gas can remain during relevant timescales in stellar clusters. Future simulations should incorporate these processes to determine whether gas persists during core collapse in more realistic scenarios, thereby validating our findings. It is also necessary to use more realistic initial conditions (e.g., IMFs, cosmological initial conditions) instead of toy models, and more efficient N-body codes to simulate larger systems.

The IMBHs formed in our simulations could serve as seeds that subsequently grow to SMBH masses via gas accretion and mergers, as suggested by recent studies \citep{inayoshi2022rapid,kritos2023massive,chung2026dense}. We conclude that gas is a key ingredient in the collisional runaway scenario for SMBH seed formation. Its inclusion in future simulations is therefore essential for a complete understanding of the formation of SMBH in NSCs.

	\section{Derivation of Critical Masses for Collisions and Relaxation}
	
	In this section we derive two quantities relevant for the dynamical evolution of stellar clusters: the critical mass for collisions (following \citet{escala2021observational}) and the relaxation mass. For the critical mass, we first present the original derivation, then extend it to include an external potential (following \citet{reinoso2020effects}), and finally generalize it by including gravitational focusing. For the relaxation mass, we derive an analogous mass scale from the two-body relaxation timescale.
	
	\subsection{Critical Mass in the Simplified Case}
	\label{sec:Mcrit_first}
	
	First, we show the derivation of the critical mass from \citet{escala2021observational}. We begin with the collisional timescale (Equation~\eqref{eq:tcoll_escala}) from Section~\ref{sec:2.1_Mcrit}, for a star cluster of equal stars with mass $m$ and radius $r_{\star}$, total mass $M$ and characteristic radius $R$:
	
	\begin{equation}
		t_{\rm coll} = \frac{1}{n \Sigma_0}\sqrt{\frac{R}{GM}}~,
	\end{equation}
	
	where $\Sigma_0=16\sqrt{\pi}r_{\star}^2(1+\Theta)$ is the effective cross-section, $n$ is the number density, and
	
	\begin{equation}
		\Theta = 9.54 \frac{m}{M_{\odot}} \frac{R_{\odot}}{r_{\star}} \left(\frac{100~{\rm km}~{\rm s}^{-1}}{\sigma}\right)^2 ,
	\end{equation}
	
	is the Safronov number. This derivation relies on two assumptions. First, we assume the system is virialized, so the velocity dispersion $\sigma$ can be expressed as a function of the total mass $M$ and characteristic radius $R$. Second, we assume $\sigma \sim 100~{\rm km}~{\rm s}^{-1}$, then the Safronov number depends only on the stellar mass and radius. The implication of this assumption is that the effective cross-section depends only on the geometric cross-section of the stars, effectively neglecting gravitational focusing (i.e., the dependence on the velocity dispersion). Collisions become dynamically important when $t_{\rm coll} \leq t_{\rm H}$:
	
	\begin{align}
		t_{\rm coll} =& \frac{1}{n \Sigma_0}\sqrt{\frac{R}{GM}}\\
		=& \frac{4\pi R^3m}{3M \Sigma_0}\sqrt{\frac{R}{GM}}\\
		=& \frac{4\pi R^{7/2}m}{3M^{3/2} \Sigma_0 G^{1/2}} \leq t_{\rm H}~,
	\end{align}
	
	where $n=3M/(4\pi R^3m)$ is the number density for an homogeneous system. Solving for $M$:
	
	\begin{align}
		M^{3/2} \geq& \frac{4\pi R^{7/2}m}{3t_{\rm H} \Sigma_0 G^{1/2}} \\
		M \geq& \left(\frac{4 \pi m}{3 \Sigma_0 G^{1/2} t_{\rm H}}\right)^{2/3}R^{7/3}~.
	\end{align}
	
	The critical mass is then:
	
	\begin{equation}
		M_{\rm crit} = \left(\frac{4 \pi m}{3 \Sigma_0 G^{1/2} t_{\rm H}}\right)^{2/3}R^{7/3}~.
	\end{equation}
	
	This defines the mass limit for a virialized system of characteristic radius $R$, composed of equal-mass stars (mass $m$, radius $r_{\star}$), for which the collision time equals the characteristic time ($t_{\rm coll} = t_{\rm H}$). If a system exceeds this mass, its collision time is shorter than $t_{\rm H}$. This limit can be represented in a radius–mass diagram \citep{escala2021observational,vergara2023global}.
	
	\subsection{Effect of an External Gravitational Potential}
	
	We now show how the critical mass changes when an external potential is considered. When no external potential is present, the virial theorem gives a relation for the time average kinetic and potential energies ($K$ and $W$):
	
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:virial_equation}
		2K+W=0~.
	\end{equation}
	
	Although $K$ and $W$ are time-average quantities, in virial equilibrium they fluctuate about their mean values (for more details see~\citet{collins1978virial}). Then, we can express $K$ and $W$ in terms of characteristic quantities:
	
	\begin{align}
		K =& \frac{1}{2} \langle \sum_{i} m_i v_i^2 \rangle  = \frac{M}{2} \langle v^2 \rangle \\
		W =& - \frac{1}{2} \langle \sum_{i, j \neq i} \frac{G m_i m_j}{|r_i - r_j|} \rangle = - \frac{G M^2}{2R_{\rm vir}}~.
	\end{align}
	
	Note that $m_i$ is the mass of the $i$--th star, and $M = \sum_{i} m_i$ is the total mass of the stellar system. For a stellar system, it is standard to replace $\langle v^2 \rangle$ with the square of the velocity dispersion, $\sigma^2$. The quantity $R_{\rm vir}$ is known as the virial radius (for consistency, we use $R$ instead of $R_{\rm vir}$ in the following). Replacing in Equation~\eqref{eq:virial_equation}, we have:
	
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{M}{2} \sigma^2 = \frac{GM^2}{2 R}~.
	\end{equation}
	
	The relation between $\sigma$ and $R_{\rm vir}$ is then:
	
	\begin{equation}
		\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{R}}~.
	\end{equation}
	
	Following \citet{reinoso2020effects}, if we consider an external potential of mass $M_{\rm ext}$ with the same spatial distribution as the stellar system, then the total mass can be rewritten as $M + M_{\rm ext}$. Assuming the stars are in global virial equilibrium with this external potential yields:
	
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{M}{2} \sigma_{\rm ext}^2 = \frac{G(M + M_{\rm ext})^2}{2 R}~.
	\end{equation}
	
	Defining $q=M_{\rm ext}/M$,
	
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:disp_vel_ext}
		\sigma_{\rm ext} = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{R}}(1 + q)~.
	\end{equation}
	
	Then, for an external potential, the collision time is:
	
	\begin{align}
		t_{\rm coll} =& \frac{1}{n \Sigma_0}\sqrt{\frac{R}{GM}}\frac{1}{(1 + q)}\\
		=& \frac{4\pi R^3m}{3M \Sigma_0}\sqrt{\frac{R}{GM}}\frac{1}{(1 + q)}\\
		=& \frac{4\pi R^{7/2}m}{3M^{3/2} \Sigma_0 G^{1/2} (1 + q)}~,
		\label{eq:t_coll_ext}
	\end{align}
	
	and the critical mass can be written as
	
	\begin{equation}
		M_{\rm crit, ext} = \left(\frac{4 \pi m}{3 \Sigma_0 G^{1/2} t_{\rm H}(1+q)}\right)^{2/3}R^{7/3}~.
	\end{equation}
	
	\subsection{Generalized Critical Mass Including Gravitational Focusing}
	
	In Section~\ref{sec:Mcrit_first}, we assumed $\sigma \sim 100~{\rm km}~{\rm s}^{-1}$. Consequently, the Safronov number depended only on the stellar mass and radius. To account for gravitational focusing, we now derive the critical mass using the virial velocity dispersion. In the following, we use $M$ to denote the mass of the stellar cluster. Then, the total mass of the system is $M(1+q)$ when considering an external potential (with $q=M_{\rm ext}/M$), and $M$ when no external potential is present. First, we expand the effective cross section:
	
	\begin{align}
		\Sigma_0 =& 16\sqrt{\pi}r_{\star}^2(1+\Theta) \\
		=& 16\sqrt{\pi}r_{\star}^2\left(1+9.54 \frac{m}{M_{\odot}} \frac{R_{\odot}}{r_{\star}} \left(\frac{100~{\rm km}~{\rm s}^{-1}}{\sigma_{\rm ext}}\right)^2\right) \\
		=& 16\sqrt{\pi} r_\star^2 + \frac{9.54\times16\sqrt{\pi} ~r_\star~ mR_\odot v_{100}^2}{M_\odot} \frac{R}{G~M(1+q)^2}~.
		\label{eq:sigma_eff_expanded}
	\end{align}
	
	We have introduced $\sigma_{\rm ext}$, and have defined $v_{100} = 100~{\rm km}~{\rm s}^{-1}$. From $t_{\rm coll} = t_{\rm H}$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:t_coll_ext}, we have
	
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:Sigma_Mstar}
		\Sigma_0~M^{3/2} = \frac{4 \pi m}{3 G^{1/2}t_H} \frac{R^{7/2}}{(1+q)}~.
	\end{equation}
	
	Replacing Equation~\eqref{eq:sigma_eff_expanded} into \eqref{eq:Sigma_Mstar} yields,
	
	
	\begin{align}
		16\sqrt{\pi} r_\star^2~M^{3/2} + \frac{9.54\times16\sqrt{\pi} ~r_\star~ mR_\odot v_{100}^2}{M_\odot} \frac{R~M^{1/2}}{G(1+q)^2} =& \frac{4 \pi m}{3 G^{1/2}t_H} \frac{R^{7/2}}{(1+q)} \\
		M^{3/2} + \frac{9.54 ~ mR_\odot v_{100}^2 R}{r_{\star} G M_{\odot} (1+q)^2}~M^{1/2} =& \frac{\sqrt{\pi} m}{12 G^{1/2}t_H r_\star^2} \frac{R^{7/2}}{(1+q)}~.
		\label{eq:equation_mcrit_poly}
	\end{align}
	
	Now, defining the coefficients
	
	\begin{align}
		A &= \frac{9.54 ~ mR_\odot v_{100}^2}{r_\star G M_\odot (1+q)^2}R \\
		B &= \frac{\sqrt{\pi} m}{12 G^{1/2}t_H r_\star^2 (1+q)}R^{7/2}~,
	\end{align}
	
	we can rewrite Equation~\eqref{eq:equation_mcrit_poly}:
	
	\begin{equation}
		M^{3/2} + A M^{1/2} = B~.
	\end{equation}
	
	To continue, we introduce the change of variable $u=M^{1/2}$:
	
	\begin{equation}
		u^3 + A u - B = 0~.
	\end{equation}
	
	Note that $u$ must be positive since $M > 0$. As $A$ and $B$ are positive, the function $f(u) = u^3 + Au - B$ ranges from $-B$ to $\infty$. Then, $f(u)$ is strictly increasing, and there is exactly one real solution. Using Cardano's formula, the real solution for a depressed cubic equation is,
	
	\begin{equation}
		u_{\rm real} = \sqrt[3]{\frac{B}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{B^2}{4} + \frac{A^3}{27}}} + \sqrt[3]{\frac{B}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{B^2}{4} + \frac{A^3}{27}}}~.
	\end{equation}
	
	Returning to $M = u^2$, we obtain
	
	\begin{align}
		M &= u_{\rm real}^2 \\ 
		&= \left(\frac{B}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{B^2}{4} + \frac{A^3}{27}}\right)^{2/3} + \left(\frac{B}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{B^2}{4} + \frac{A^3}{27}}\right)^{2/3} \\
		&\quad + 2\left( \frac{B^2}{4} - \left(\frac{B^2}{4} + \frac{A^3}{27}\right) \right)^{1/3}, \\
		&= \left(\frac{B}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{B^2}{4} + \frac{A^3}{27}}\right)^{2/3} + \left(\frac{B}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{B^2}{4} + \frac{A^3}{27}}\right)^{2/3} - \frac{2A}{3}~.
	\end{align}
	
	Defining $\alpha = A/R$ and $\beta = B/R^{7/2}$, the critical mass is:
	
	\begin{equation}
		M_{\rm crit} = \left(\frac{\beta R^{7/2}}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{\beta^2 R^7}{4} + \frac{\alpha^3 R^3}{27}}\right)^{2/3} + \left(\frac{\beta R^{7/2}}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{\beta^2 R^7}{4} + \frac{\alpha^3 R^3}{27}}\right)^{2/3} - \frac{2\alpha R}{3}~.
		\label{eq:Mcrit_extended}
	\end{equation}
	
	Equation~\eqref{eq:Mcrit_extended} sets the upper mass limit for a stellar system of mass $M$, embedded in and in virial equilibrium with an external potential ( with mass $M_{\rm ext} = q M$), such that its collision timescale satisfies $t_{\rm coll} = t_{\rm H}$. In consequence, if $M > M_{\rm crit}$, the collision time is shorter than $t_{\rm H}$.
	
	\subsection{Derivation of the Relaxation Mass}
	\label{sec:app_Mrelax}
	
	In this section, we derive a similar relation for the relaxation timescale $t_{\rm relax}$. In a stellar cluster, the energy of stars changes due to stellar encounters, which are typically divided into strong and weak encounters. If we consider a target star with velocity $v$, a strong encounter is one in which the change in velocity $\delta v$ is comparable to $v$ (i.e., $\delta v \sim v$). On the other hand, in a weak encounter, the change in velocity is small ($\delta v \ll v$). While strong encounters can produce notable effects (e.g., stellar collisions, binary formation), they are less frequent. Weak encounters, however, can accumulate over time and significantly alter the energy distribution and trajectories of stars. The relaxation timescale is the time required for a typical star's velocity to change significantly due to the cumulative effect of weak encounters. From \citet{2008gady.book.....B}, the deflection in the star velocity for crossing the system once is:
	
	\begin{equation}
		\delta v^2 \simeq 8N \left(\frac{Gm}{Rv}\right)^2 \ln(\Lambda)~,
	\end{equation}
	
	where $N$ is the number of stars, $m$ and $v$ are the mass and velocity of the target star, and $\Lambda = R/b_{90}$ is the ratio of the maximum to minimum impact parameter for weak encounters. From \citet{2008gady.book.....B}, the minimum impact parameter is $b_{90} = 2Gm/v^2$, then $\Lambda = v^2 R/(2Gm)$. The number of system crossings required to change $v$ significantly is
	
	\begin{equation}
		n_{\rm cross} = \frac{v^2}{\delta v^2} = \frac{1}{8N\ln(\Lambda)}\left(\frac{Rv^2}{Gm}\right)^2~.
	\end{equation}
	
	When an external potential is present, we can approximate $v^2 \sim \sigma_{\rm ext}^2$, and $n_{\rm cross}$ becomes
	
	\begin{align}
		n_{\rm cross} =& \frac{1}{8N\ln(\frac{GmN(1+q)^2}{R} \frac{R}{2Gm})}\left(\frac{R}{Gm} \frac{GmN(1+q)^2}{R}\right)^2 \\
		\simeq& \frac{0.1 N(1+q)^4}{\ln(N(1+q)^2)}~.
		\label{eq:n_cross}
	\end{align}
	
	In the last equation, we have used $M = Nm$, and we have neglected the $1/2$ inside $\ln(\Lambda)$. In the absence of an external potential (i.e., $q=0$), Equation~\eqref{eq:n_cross} reduces to the standard expression from \citet{2008gady.book.....B}. Since a typical star crosses the system in a crossing time $t_{\rm cross}$, the relaxation timescale is:
	
	\begin{equation}
		t_{\rm relax} = n_{\rm cross} t_{\rm cross} = \frac{0.1 N(1+q)^4}{\ln(N(1+q)^2)} t_{\rm cross}~.
		\label{eq:trelax_tcross}
	\end{equation}
	
	With the crossing time estimated as $t_{\rm cross} = R/\sigma$, and using $\sigma_{\rm ext}$, Equation~\eqref{eq:trelax_tcross} becomes
	
	\begin{equation}
		t_{\rm relax} = \frac{0.1 N(1+q)^3}{\ln(N(1+q)^2)} \frac{R^{3/2}}{\sqrt{GM}}~.
		\label{eq:trelax_ext}
	\end{equation}
	
	Imposing $t_{\rm relax} < t_{\rm H}$ yields a relation between the radius $R$ and mass $M$:
	
	\begin{align}
		R^{3/2} \leq & \frac{t_{\rm H} \ln(N(1+q)^2) \sqrt{GM}}{0.1 N(1+q)^3 }\\
		\leq & \frac{t_{\rm H} m \ln(M(1+q)^2/m) \sqrt{GM}}{0.1 M(1+q)^3} \\ 
		=& \frac{t_{\rm H}m}{0.1(1+q)^3} \ln(\frac{M(1+q)^2}{m}) \sqrt{\frac{G}{M}} \\
		R \leq& \left(\frac{t_{\rm H}m}{0.1(1+q)^3} \ln(\frac{M(1+q)^2}{m})\right)^{2/3} \left(\frac{G}{M}\right)^{1/3}~.
		\label{eq:R_relax}
	\end{align}
	
	Equation~\eqref{eq:R_relax} defines the condition for a system to significantly alter its initial conditions on a timescale $\sim t_{\rm H}$. If we denote the equality case by $R_{\rm relax}$, systems with $R > R_{\rm relax}$ have a relaxation timescale longer than $t_{\rm H}$. In a collisionless scenario, such systems are considered to be in virial equilibrium.
	
	We now derive a mass scale from the condition in Equation~\eqref{eq:R_relax}. First, we rearrange the terms in \eqref{eq:R_relax} for the limit case:
	
	\begin{align}
		R =& \left(\frac{t_{\rm H}m}{0.1(1+q)^3} \ln(\frac{M(1+q)^2}{m})\right)^{2/3} \left(\frac{G}{M}\right)^{1/3} \\
		R^3 M =& \left(\frac{t_{\rm H}m}{0.1(1+q)^3} \ln(\frac{M(1+q)^2}{m})\right)^2 G~.
	\end{align}
	
	We introduce the change of variable $u\equiv M(1+q)^2/m$,
	
	\begin{align}
		\frac{R^3um}{(1+q)^2} =& \left(\frac{t_{\rm H}m}{0.1(1+q)^3} \ln(u)\right)^2 G \\
		\frac{R^{3/2}u^{1/2}m^{1/2}}{1+q} =& \frac{t_{\rm H}m}{0.1(1+q)^3} \ln(u) G^{1/2} \\
		u^{1/2} =& \frac{t_{\rm H}\sqrt{Gm}}{0.1(1+q)^2R^{3/2}} \ln(u)~.
	\end{align}
	
	Defining $\lambda = (t_{\rm H}\sqrt{Gm})/(0.1(1+q)^2R^{3/2})$, 
	
	\begin{equation}
		u^{1/2} = \lambda \ln(u)~,
	\end{equation}
	
	and introducing another change of variable, $\omega = \ln(u)$:
	
	\begin{align}
		e^{\omega/2} =& \lambda \omega \\
		\frac{1}{\lambda} =& \omega e^{-\omega/2} \\
		\frac{-1}{2\lambda} =& \frac{- \omega}{2}e^{-\omega/2}~.
		\label{eq:omega_lambert}
	\end{align}
	
	Equation~\eqref{eq:omega_lambert} is solved by the Lambert $W$ function \citep{weisstein2002lambert}. Indeed, for a function $f(w)=we^w$,  its inverse is the Lambert $W(z)$ function, defined such that $W(f(w)) = w$. Hence,
	
	\begin{align}
		\frac{-\omega}{2} =& W\left(\frac{-1}{2\lambda}\right) \\
		\omega = -2 W\left(\frac{-1}{2\lambda}\right)~.
	\end{align}
	
	Returning to $u = e^{\omega}$,
	
	\begin{equation}
		u = \exp(-2 W\left(\frac{-1}{2\lambda}\right))~.
	\end{equation}
	
	Finally, we use $M = mu/(1+q)^2$:
	
	\begin{equation}
		M_{\rm relax} = \frac{m}{(1+q)^2}\exp(-2 W\left(\frac{-1}{2\lambda}\right))~.
		\label{eq:Mrelax_lambert}
	\end{equation}
	
	Since $-1/2\lambda < 0$ ($\lambda > 0$), the argument of the Lambert $W$ function is negative, and there are two real-valued branches \citep[Chapter~III.17]{higham2015princeton}. The principal branch, $W_{0}(z)$, is defined for $z \in [-1/e, \infty)$, and takes values $W_{0}(z)> -1$. In particular, for $z \in [-1/e, 0]$, it ranges from $-1$ to $0$. The lower branch, $W_{-1}(z)$, is defined for $z \in [-1/e, 0]$, and ranges from $-1$ to $-\infty$, with $\lim_{z \to 0^-} W_{-1}(z) = -\infty$. Figure~\ref{fig:Lambert_function_branches} shows the principal and lower branches of the Lambert function.
	
	\begin{figure}[ht]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{img/Plots/lambert_plot.png}
		\caption{Lambert function. The black line shows the $W_{0}(z)$ branch, and the blue line shows the $W_{-1}(z)$ branch.}
		\label{fig:Lambert_function_branches}
	\end{figure} 
	
	If we consider the principal branch $W_{0}(z)$, we note that the exponential in Equation~\eqref{eq:Mrelax_lambert} takes values between $e^0$ and $e^2$. Consequently, the relaxation mass would be of the order of the individual stellar mass ($M_{\rm relax} \sim m$). In contrast, for the lower branch $W_{-1}(z)$, the exponential ranges from $e^2$ to $\infty$, yielding a relaxation mass that can reach the mass scale of the system. We therefore adopt the lower branch as the physically relevant solution:
	
	\begin{equation}
		M_{\rm relax} = \frac{m}{(1+q)^2}\exp(-2 W_{-1}\left(\frac{-1}{2\lambda}\right))~.
		\label{eq:Mrelax_lambert_lower}
	\end{equation}
	
	Finally, we must verify that the argument of the Lambert $W$ function lies within the domain of the lower branch: $-1/e \leq -1/2\lambda < 0$. The right inequality is satisfied since $\lambda > 0$. The left part gives a condition over the characteristic timescale:
	
	\begin{align}
		-1/e \leq& -1/2\lambda \\
		-2\lambda \leq& -e \\
		\lambda \geq& e/2 \\
		\frac{t_{\rm H}\sqrt{Gm}}{0.1(1+q)^2R^{3/2}} \geq& \frac{e}{2} \\
		t_{\rm H} \geq& \frac{0.1e(1+q)^2R^{3/2}}{2\sqrt{Gm}}
		\label{eq:tH_condition}		
	\end{align}
	
	Equation~\eqref{eq:tH_condition} implies that, for a system of characteristic radius $R$ composed of equal-mass stars $m$, there exists a minimum possible relaxation time. From Equation~\eqref{eq:trelax_ext}, for fixed $R$ and $m$, the number of stars that minimizes $t_{\rm relax}$ is $N_{\rm opt}=e^2/(1+q)^2$. If we evaluate $t_{\rm relax}(N_{\rm opt})$:
	
	\begin{align}
		t_{\rm relax}(N_{\rm opt}) =& \frac{0.1 N_{\rm opt}(1+q)^3}{\ln(N_{\rm opt}(1+q)^2)} \frac{R^{3/2}}{\sqrt{GN_{\rm opt}m}} \\
		=& \frac{0.1 e^2(1+q)^{-2}(1+q)^3}{\ln(e^2(1+q)^{-2}(1+q)^2)} \frac{R^{3/2}}{\sqrt{Ge^2(1+q)^{-2}m}}\\
		=& \frac{0.1 e^2(1+q)}{2} \frac{R^{3/2}(1+q)}{\sqrt{Gm}e}\\
		=& \frac{0.1 e(1+q)^2 R^{3/2}}{2\sqrt{Gm}}~.
	\end{align}
	
	\section{Fourth-Order Hermite Integration Scheme} 
	\label{ap:A}
	
	The N-body code used in this work, \texttt{ph4}, is based on a fourth-order Hermite integrator. The following subsections describe in detail the individual time step scheme (ITS) from \citet{makino1992}, including the time step criterion in the N-body code.
	
	% Primer paso
	\subsection{Step 1: Particle selection}
	\label{ap:A.1}
	The first step is to select the next particle to be advanced. At any given time, each particle $j$ has its own mass $m_j$, time $t_j$, time step $\Delta t_j$, coordinates $\mathbf{x}_j$ and $\mathbf{v}_j$, acceleration $\mathbf{a}_j$ and jerk $\dot{\mathbf{a}}_j$ (with $\dot{\mathbf{a}}_j = \partial \mathbf{a}_j / \partial t$). The selected particle $i$ is the one satisfying the condition:
	
	\begin{equation}
		i = \arg\min_j(t_j + \Delta t_j)
	\end{equation}
	
	Note that always $t_j \leq t_i + \Delta t_i$ for all $j$. The current simulation time is then set to $t = t_i + \Delta t_i$.
	
	% Segundo paso
	\subsection{Step 2: Calculate the predicted position and velocities for all particles}
	\label{ap:A.2}
	
	The positions and velocities of all particles are predicted at time $t$, using a Taylor expansion up to third-order (jerk):
	\begin{align}
		\mathbf{x}_{p, j} &= \mathbf{x}_j + \mathbf{v}_j (t - t_j) + \frac{\mathbf{a}_{0,j}}{2!} (t - t_j)^2 + \frac{\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{0,j}}{3!} (t - t_j)^3 \label{eq:ap_xpred} \\
		\mathbf{v}_{p, j} &= \mathbf{v}_j + \mathbf{a}_{0,j} (t - t_j) + \frac{\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{0,j}}{2!} (t - t_j)^2 \label{eq:ap_velpred}
	\end{align}
	
	The acceleration is due only to gravitational force. The expression for each particle $j$ is:
	
	\begin{equation}
		\mathbf{a}_{0, j} = \sum_{k \neq j} G m_k \frac{\mathbf{r}_{0,jk}}{(\mathbf{r}_{0,jk}^2 + \epsilon^2)^{3/2}} \label{eq:ap_accel}
	\end{equation}
	
	where $\epsilon$ is the softening parameter, and
	
	\begin{equation}
		\mathbf{r}_{0,jk} = \mathbf{r}_{k} - \mathbf{r}_{j}
	\end{equation}
	
	Differentiating Eq.~\eqref{eq:ap_accel} with respect to time yields the jerk:
	
	\begin{align}
		\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{0, j} &= \frac{\partial \mathbf{a}_j}{\partial t} \\
		&= \sum_{k \neq j} G m_k \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\frac{\mathbf{r}_{0,jk}}{(\mathbf{r}_{0,jk}^2 + \epsilon^2)^{3/2}} \\
		\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{0, j} &= \sum_{k \neq j} G m_k \left[\frac{\mathbf{v}_{0,jk}}{(\mathbf{r}_{0,jk}^2 + \epsilon^2)^{3/2}} - \frac{3(\mathbf{v}_{0,jk} \cdot \mathbf{r}_{0,jk})\mathbf{r}_{0,jk}}{(\mathbf{r}_{0,jk}^2 + \epsilon^2)^{5/2}}\right] \label{eq:ap_jerk}
	\end{align}
	
	where
	
	\begin{equation}
		\mathbf{v}_{0,jk} = \mathbf{v}_{k} - \mathbf{v}_{j}
	\end{equation}
	
	% Tercer paso
	\subsection{Step 3: Calculate acceleration and jerk for particle $i$}
	\label{ap:A.3}
	
	Similar to equations (\ref{eq:ap_accel}) and (\ref{eq:ap_jerk}), the acceleration and jerk are calculated using the predicted positions and velocity:
	
	\begin{align}
		\mathbf{a}_{1, i} &= \sum_{j \neq i} G m_j \frac{\mathbf{r}_{ij}}{(\mathbf{r}_{ij}^2 + \epsilon^2)^{3/2}} \label{eq:ap_accel_pred} \\
		\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{1, i} &= \sum_{j \neq i} G m_j \left[\frac{\mathbf{v}_{ij}}{(\mathbf{r}_{ij}^2 + \epsilon^2)^{3/2}} - \frac{3(\mathbf{v}_{ij} \cdot \mathbf{r}_{ij})\mathbf{r}_{ij}}{(\mathbf{r}_{ij}^2 + \epsilon^2)^{5/2}}\right] \label{eq:ap_jerk_pred} \\
		\mathbf{r}_{ij} &= \mathbf{r}_{p,j} - \mathbf{r}_{p,i} \\
		\mathbf{v}_{ij} &= \mathbf{v}_{p,j} - \mathbf{v}_{p,i}
	\end{align}
	
	In this notation, the subscript $0$ denotes quantities evaluated at the beginning of the time step ($t_j$), while the subscript $1$ denotes quantities evaluated at the end of the time step ($t$), using the predicted position and velocity ($\mathbf{x}_{p, i}$ and $\mathbf{v}_{p_i}$).
	
	% Cuarto Paso
	\subsection{Step 4: Second and third time derivative of acceleration, using Hermite interpolation}
	\label{ap:A.4}
	
	To obtain a high-order correction of $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{v}$, the acceleration of particle $i$ is modeled as a third-order polynomial in time:
	
	\begin{equation}
		\mathbf{a}(\tau) = \alpha \tau^3 + \beta \tau^2 + \gamma \tau + \delta \label{eq:ap_accel_pol}
	\end{equation}
	
	The successive time derivatives of $\mathbf{a}(\tau)$ are then:
	
	\begin{align}
		\dot{\mathbf{a}}(\tau) &= 3 \alpha \tau^2 + 2 \beta \tau + \gamma \label{eq:ap_jerk_pol} \\
		\mathbf{a}^{(2)} (\tau) &= 6 \alpha \tau + 2 \beta \label{eq:ap_a2_pol} \\
		\mathbf{a}^{(3)} (\tau) &= 6 \alpha \label{eq:ap_a3_pol}
	\end{align}
	
	The coefficients $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$, and $\delta$ are determined by using the known values of acceleration and jerk at times $t_i$ and $t$. This provides a system of four equations:
	
	\begin{align}
		\mathbf{a}(t_i) = \mathbf{a}_{0, i} &=  \alpha t_i^3 + \beta t_i^2 + \gamma t_i + \delta \label{eq:ap_a_ti} \\
		\mathbf{a}(t) = \mathbf{a}_{1, i} &=  \alpha t^3 + \beta t^2 + \gamma t + \delta \label{eq:ap_a_t} \\
		\dot{\mathbf{a}}(t_i) = \dot{\mathbf{a}}_{0, i} &= 3 \alpha t_i^2 + 2 \beta t_i + \gamma \label{eq:ap_adot_ti} \\
		\dot{\mathbf{a}}(t) = \dot{\mathbf{a}}_{1, i} &= 3 \alpha t^2 + 2 \beta t + \gamma \label{eq:ap_adot_t}
	\end{align}
	
	where $t = t_i + \Delta t_i$ is the current time. As the system of equations is linearly independent, the four coefficients can be determined. However, determining $\alpha$ and $\beta$ is enough to get the second and third time derivative of acceleration. From (\ref{eq:ap_adot_t}):
	
	\begin{equation}
		\gamma = \dot{\mathbf{a}}_{1, i} - 3 \alpha t^2 - 2 \beta t \label{eq:app_gamma}
	\end{equation}
	
	Subtracting Eq.~\eqref{eq:ap_adot_ti} from \eqref{eq:ap_adot_t}:
	
	\begin{equation}
		\beta = \frac{(\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{1, i} - \dot{\mathbf{a}}_{0, i}) - \alpha 3 (t^2 - t_i^2)}{2(t - t_i)} \label{eq:ap_b_var}
	\end{equation}
	
	Subtracting Eq.~\eqref{eq:ap_a_ti} from \eqref{eq:ap_a_t}, and using results from \eqref{eq:app_gamma} and \eqref{eq:ap_b_var} gives an expression for $\alpha$:
	
	\begin{equation}
		\alpha = \frac{(\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{1, i} + \dot{\mathbf{a}}_{0, i})(t - t_i) - 2(\mathbf{a}_{1, i} - \mathbf{a}_{0, i})}{(t - t_i)^3} \label{eq:ap_alpha_var}
	\end{equation}
	
	Replacing this result in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ap_b_var}:
	
	\begin{equation}
		\beta = \frac{-\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{1, i}(t + 2 t_i) - \dot{\mathbf{a}}_{0, i}(2 t + t_i)}{(t - t_i)^2} + \frac{3(\mathbf{a}_{1, i} - \mathbf{a}_{0, i})(t + t_i)}{(t - t_i)^3}
	\end{equation}
	
	The second and third time derivatives of the acceleration at time $t_i$ can now be evaluated. For the Eq.~\eqref{eq:ap_a3_pol}, and using $t - t_i = \Delta t_i$:
	
	\begin{align}
		\mathbf{a}^{(3)}_{0, i} &= \mathbf{a}^{(3)} (t_i) = 6 \alpha \\
		\mathbf{a}^{(3)}_{0, i} &= 6 \frac{(\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{1, i} + \dot{\mathbf{a}}_{0, i})\Delta t_i - 2(\mathbf{a}_{1, i} - \mathbf{a}_{0, i})}{{\Delta t_i}^3} \\
		\mathbf{a}^{(3)}_{0, i} &= \frac{12 (\mathbf{a}_{0, i} - \mathbf{a}_{1, i}) + 6(\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{0, i} + \dot{\mathbf{a}}_{1, i})\Delta t_i}{{\Delta t_i}^3} \label{eq:ap_a3_ti}
	\end{align}
	
	Evaluating the second time derivative in $t_i$, and replacing the obtained for $\alpha$ and $\beta$:
	
	\begin{align}
		\mathbf{a}^{(2)}_{0, i} &= \mathbf{a}^{(2)} (t_i) = 6 \alpha t_i + \beta \\
		\mathbf{a}^{(2)}_{0, i} &= \frac{-6(\mathbf{a}_{0, i} - \mathbf{a}_{1, i}) - \Delta t_i(4\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{0, i} + 2\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{1, i})}{{\Delta t_i}^2}
	\end{align}
	
	
	% Quinto Paso
	\subsection{Step 5: Add corrections}
	\label{ap:A.5}
	
	The next step is to correct the position and velocity of particle $i$ at time $t$. Using a higher-order Taylor expansion around $t_i$:
	
	\begin{align}
		\mathbf{x}_i(t_i + \Delta t_i) =& \mathbf{x}_i(t_i) + \dot{\mathbf{x}_i}(t_i)\Delta t_i + \frac{{\mathbf{x}_i}^{(2)}(t_i){\Delta t_i}^2}{2!} + ... + \frac{{\mathbf{x}_i}^{(5)}(t_i){\Delta t_i}^5}{5!}\\
		=& \mathbf{x}_i + \mathbf{v}_i \Delta t_i + \frac{\mathbf{a}_{0, i} {\Delta t_i}^2}{2} + \frac{\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{0, i} {\Delta t_i}^3}{6} + \frac{\mathbf{a}^{(2)}_{0, i} {\Delta t_i}^4}{24} + \frac{\mathbf{a}^{(3)}_{0, i} {\Delta t_i}^5}{120}
	\end{align}
	
	The first four terms of this expansion correspond to the predicted position $\mathbf{x}_{p, i}$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:ap_xpred}. Therefore, the corrected position can be written as:
	
	\begin{equation}
		\mathbf{x}_i(t_i + \Delta t_i) = \mathbf{x}_{p, i} + \frac{\mathbf{a}^{(2)}_{0, i} {\Delta t_i}^4}{24} + \frac{\mathbf{a}^{(3)}_{0, i} {\Delta t_i}^5}{120} \label{eq:ap_xcorr}
	\end{equation}
	
	In a similar way, the corrected velocity is:
	
	\begin{equation}
		\mathbf{v}_i(t_i + \Delta t_i) = \mathbf{v}_{p, i} + \frac{\mathbf{a}^{(2)}_{0, i} {\Delta t_i}^3}{6} + \frac{\mathbf{a}^{(3)}_{0, i} {\Delta t_i}^4}{24} \label{eq:ap_vcorr}
	\end{equation}
	
	The advantage of this scheme is that achieving high integration accuracy only requires the explicit calculation of the acceleration and jerk for all particles, and the predicted acceleration and jerk for the particle being advanced. This results in an integration scheme that is fourth-order accurate in time ($\mathcal{O}(a^{(3)})$).
	
	\subsection{Step 6: Time step update}
	\label{ap:A.6}
	
	After updating the position and velocity of particle $i$, a new time step $\Delta t_i$ must be calculated. This step is crucial for controlling the integration error. A proven and stable criterion is given by the standard formula \citet{aarseth1985}:
	
	\begin{equation}
		\Delta t_i = \sqrt{\eta \frac{|\mathbf{a}_{1, i}| |\mathbf{a}^{(2)}_{1, i}| + |\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{1, i}|^2}{|\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{1, i}| |\mathbf{a}^{(3)}_{1, i}| + |\mathbf{a}^{(2)}_{1, i}|^2}} \label{eq:ap_tstep_std}
	\end{equation}
	
	The values for $\mathbf{a}_{1, i}$ and $\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{1, i}$ are known from the direct calculation in Step 3. The value of $\mathbf{a}^{(3)}(t)$ is constant, since a third-order polynomial interpolation is used. Only $\mathbf{a}^{(2)}_{1, i}$ should be calculated:
	
	\begin{equation}
		\mathbf{a}^{(2)}_{1, i} = \mathbf{a}^{(2)}_{0, i} + \Delta t_i \mathbf{a}^{(3)}_{0, i}
	\end{equation}
	
	During the initialization of the algorithm, the higher-order derivatives of the acceleration are not available, so an alternative formula for time step can be used in the startup:
	
	\begin{equation}
		\Delta t = \eta_s \frac{|\mathbf{a}|}{|\dot{\mathbf{a}}|} \label{eq:ap_tstep_ini}
	\end{equation}
	
	The suggested value for the startup parameter is $\eta_s \sim 0.01$ \citep{makino1992}.
	
	\section{SPH equations}
	\label{ap:SPH_equations}
	In this section, we derive the hydrodynamic equations for the SPH method. We follow the derivation of \citet{springel2002cosmological}. From a particle perspective, the idea of SPH is to use a discrete representation of the Lagrangian \citep{rasio2000particle}:
	
	\begin{equation}
		L_{\rm SPH} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} m_i \left(\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{v}^2_i - u_i \right)~.
		\label{eq:Lagrangian_SPH}
	\end{equation}
	
	The sum is over all the particles (or fluid elements), described by a mass $m_i$, velocity $\mathbf{v}_i = d \mathbf{r}_i/dt$ and position $\mathbf{r}_i$. For Equation~\ref{eq:Lagrangian_SPH} to be the Lagrangian of a fluid, it is necessary to have an adequate prescription to compute the density $\rho_i$ of any particle. In SPH, the density is calculated as
	
	\begin{equation}
		\rho_i = \rho(\mathbf{r}_i) = \sum_{j} m_j W(|\mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j|;h_i)~,
	\end{equation}
	
	where $W$ is the smoothing kernel, and $h_i$ is the smoothing length parameter. In particular, the kernel used in FI is the cubic spline kernel \citep{pelupessy2005numerical,monaghan1992smoothed}:
	
	\begin{equation}
		W(r,h) = \frac{1}{\pi h^3}
		\begin{cases}
			1 - \frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{r}{h}\right)^2 + \frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{r}{h}\right)^3, & 0 \leq \frac{r}{h} \leq 1, \\[2ex]
			\frac{1}{4}\left(2 - \frac{r}{h}\right)^3, & 1 \leq \frac{r}{h} \leq 2, \\[2ex]
			0, & \text{otherwise}~.
		\end{cases}
	\end{equation}
	
	In some derivations, $h_i$ is taken as a constant. However, since different particles require different resolutions, modern codes use a variable $h_i$. Requiring that a smoothing volume contains a fixed mass leads to $M_{\rm SPH} = (4 \pi /3)h_i^3 \rho_i$, where $M_{\rm SPH} = \bar{m}N_{\rm SPH}$, $\bar{m}$ the mean mass among SPH particles and $N_{\rm SPH}$ is a fixed number of neighboring particles. The condition over $h_i$ gives the restriction:
	
	\begin{equation}
		\phi_i = \frac{4 \pi}{3}h_i^3(\rho_i + \rho_0) - M_{\rm SPH} = 0~.
	\end{equation}
	
	Here, a fixed density $\rho_0$ is introduced to prevent $h_i$ from diverging in low-density regions. The constraints can be introduced into the Lagrangian via Lagrange multipliers $\lambda_i$. Then the equations of motion yield
	
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L_{\rm SPH}}{\partial \dot{q}_i} - \frac{\partial L_{\rm SPH}}{\partial q_i} = \sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_j \frac{\partial \phi_j}{\partial q_i}~,
		\label{eq:lagrangian_equations}
	\end{equation}
	
	where $q_i$ is replaced by the coordinate, and $\dot{q}_i = d q_i/dt$. The advantage of this approach is that by treating $h_i$ as independent variables in the Lagrangian, the $\grad{h_i}$terms are consistently included in the equations of motion.
	
	\subsection{Solving $\lambda_i$}
	
	If we consider a polytropic equation of state, the pressure is given by
	
	\begin{equation}
		P=A \rho(\mathbf{r})^{\gamma}~,
	\end{equation}
	
	where $\gamma$ is the polytropic index, and $A$ is the entropic function (see \citet{horedt2004polytropes} for a complete derivation). In the SPH formalism, the pressure for a particle is
	
	\begin{equation}
		P_i=A_i \rho_i^{\gamma}~.
	\end{equation}
	
	This is the entropic equation of state. Since we are interested in energy conservation, we express $A_i$ in terms of the specific internal energy $u_i = A_i \rho_i^{\gamma - 1}/(\gamma - 1)$. Then, the pressure is
	
	\begin{equation}
		P_i= u_i \rho_i (\gamma - 1)~.
	\end{equation}
	
	First, we solve the Lagrangian equations (Equation~\eqref{eq:lagrangian_equations}) for the variable $h_i$. As there is no explicit dependency on $\dot{h}_i$, $\partial L_{\rm SPH} / \partial \dot{h}_i = 0$. Then, for $q_i=h_i$ Equation~\eqref{eq:lagrangian_equations} becomes:
	
	\begin{align}
		- \frac{\partial}{\partial h_i} \sum_{j=1}^{N} m_j \left(\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\mathbf{r}_j}|^2 - u_j \right) = & \sum_{k=1}^{N}\lambda_k \frac{\partial \phi_k}{\partial h_i} \\
		-\sum_{j=1}^{N} m_j \left(\dot{\mathbf{r}_j} \frac{\partial \dot{\mathbf{r}_j}}{\partial h_i} - \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial h_i} \right) =& \sum_{k=1}^{N}\lambda_k \frac{\partial}{\partial h_i} \left(\frac{4 \pi}{3}h_k^3(\rho_k + \rho_0) - M_{\rm SPH} \right) \\
		\sum_{j=1}^{N}m_j \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial \rho_j} \frac{\partial \rho_j}{\partial h_j} \frac{\partial h_j}{\partial h_i} =& \sum_{k=1}^{N}\lambda_k \frac{4 \pi}{3} \left(3 h_k^2(\rho_k + \rho_0) + h_k^3 \frac{\partial \rho_k}{\partial h_k} \right)\frac{\partial h_k}{\partial h_i}~.
	\end{align}
	
	Now, we use that $\partial u / \partial \rho = P/\rho^2$, and applying $\partial h_l / \partial h_m = \delta_{lm}$ in the summations:
	
	\begin{align}
		\sum_{j=1}^{N}m_j \frac{P_j}{\rho_j^2} \frac{\partial \rho_j}{\partial h_j} \delta_{ji} =& \sum_{k=1}^{N}\lambda_k \frac{4 \pi}{3} \left(3 h_k^2(\rho_k + \rho_0) + h_k^3 \frac{\partial \rho_k}{\partial h_k} \right) \delta_{ki}\\
		m_i \frac{P_i}{\rho_i^2} \frac{\partial \rho_i}{\partial h_i} =& \lambda_i \frac{4 \pi}{3} \left(3 h_i^2 (\rho_i + \rho_0) + h_i^3 \frac{\partial \rho_i}{\partial h_i}\right) \\
		=& \lambda_i \frac{4 \pi h_i^3}{3} \frac{\partial \rho_i}{\partial h_i}\left(1 + \frac{3(\rho_i + \rho_0)}{h_i}\left[\frac{\partial \rho_i}{\partial h_i}\right]^{-1}\right)~.
	\end{align}
	
	The Lagrange multipliers are then given by
	
	\begin{equation}
		\lambda_i = \frac{3}{4 \pi} \frac{m_i}{h_i^3} \frac{P_i}{\rho_i^2} \left(1 + \frac{3 (\rho_i + \rho_0)}{h_i} \left[\frac{\partial \rho_i}{\partial h_i}\right]^{-1}\right)^{-1}~.
	\end{equation}
	
	\subsection{Equation of motion for SPH particles}
	
	We now derive the equations of motion by applying Equation~\eqref{eq:lagrangian_equations} to the spatial coordinates $q_i = \mathbf{r}_i$:
	
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L_{\rm SPH}}{\partial \dot{\mathbf{r}_i}} - \frac{\partial L_{\rm SPH}}{\partial \mathbf{r}_i} = \sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_j \frac{\partial \phi_j}{\partial \mathbf{r}_i}~.
		\label{eq:Lagr_to_eq_of_motion}
	\end{equation}
	
	The first term on the left can be obtained directly:
	
	\begin{align}
		\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L_{\rm SPH}}{\partial \dot{\mathbf{r}_i}} =& \frac{d}{dt} \sum_{j=1}^{N} m_j \mathbf{v}_j \frac{\partial \mathbf{v}_j}{\partial \mathbf{v}_i} \\
		=& m_i \frac{d \mathbf{v}_i}{dt}~.
	\end{align}
	
	The second term:
	
	\begin{align}
		\frac{\partial L_{\rm SPH}}{\partial \mathbf{r}_i} =& \sum_{j=1}^{N} m_j \left(\dot{\mathbf{r}_j} \frac{\partial \dot{\mathbf{r}_j}}{\partial \mathbf{r}_j} - \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial \mathbf{r}_i} \right) \\
		=& - \sum_{j=1}^{N} m_j \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial \rho_j} \grad_i{\rho_j} \\
		=& \sum_{j=1}^{N} m_j \frac{P_j}{\rho_j^2} \grad_i{\rho_j}~,
	\end{align}
	
	where $\grad_i = \partial / \partial \mathbf{r}_i$. For the right side of Equation~\eqref{eq:Lagr_to_eq_of_motion}:
	
	\begin{align}
		\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_j \frac{\partial \phi_j}{\partial \mathbf{r}_i} =& \sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_j \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf{r}_i} \left(\frac{4 \pi}{3}h_j^3(\rho_j + \rho_0) - M_{\rm SPH}\right) \\
		=& \sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_j \frac{4 \pi}{3} h_j^3 \grad_i{\rho_j} ~.
	\end{align}
	
	Substituting all the terms back into Equation~\eqref{eq:Lagr_to_eq_of_motion},
	
	\begin{align}
		m_i \frac{d \mathbf{v}_i}{dt} - \sum_{j=1}^{N} m_j \frac{P_j}{\rho_j^2} \grad_i{\rho_j} =& \sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_j \frac{4 \pi}{3} h_j^3 \grad_i{\rho_j} \\
		m_i \frac{d \mathbf{v}_i}{dt} =& \sum_{j=1}^{N} m_j \frac{P_j}{\rho_j^2}\left[1 + \lambda_j \frac{4 \pi}{3} \frac{h_j^3}{m_j} \frac{\rho_j^2}{P_j} \right] \grad_i{\rho_j}~.
		\label{eq:C_25}
	\end{align}
	
	Expanding the term with $\lambda_i$, 
	
	\begin{align}
		\lambda_j \frac{4 \pi}{3} \frac{h_j^3}{m_j} \frac{\rho_j^2}{P_j} =& \left(1 + \frac{3 (\rho_j + \rho_0)}{h_j} \left[\frac{\partial \rho_j}{\partial h_j}\right]^{-1}\right)^{-1} \\
		=& \left(1 + \left[ \frac{h_j}{3 (\rho_j + \rho_0)}\frac{\partial \rho_j}{\partial h_j}\right]^{-1}\right)^{-1}~.
	\end{align}
	
	Defining 
	
	\begin{equation}
		f_j = \left(1 + \frac{h_j}{3 (\rho_{j} + \rho_0)} \frac{\partial \rho_j}{\partial h_j}\right)^{-1}~,
	\end{equation}
	
	then the term involving $\lambda_j$ simplifies to a compact expression, as shown below:
	
	\begin{align}
		\lambda_j \frac{4 \pi}{3} \frac{h_j^3}{m_j} \frac{\rho_j^2}{P_j} =& \left(1 + (f_j^{-1} - 1)^{-1} \right)^{-1} \\
		=& \left(\frac{f_j^{-1}}{f_j^{-1} - 1}\right)^{-1} = 1 - f_j~.
		\label{eq:SPH_m_dv_dt}
	\end{align}
	
	Replacing back to Equation~\eqref{eq:C_25},
	
	\begin{equation}
		m_i \frac{d \mathbf{v}_i}{dt} = - \sum_{j=1}^{N} m_j \frac{P_j}{\rho_j^2}f_j \grad_i{\rho_j}~.
	\end{equation}
	
	Using the notation $W_{ij}(h) = W(|\mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j|, h)$, then we can write \citep{springel2002cosmological}
	
	\begin{equation}
		\grad_i{\rho_j} = m_i \grad_i{W_{ij}(h_j)} + \delta_{ij}\sum_{k=1}^{N} m_k \grad_i{W_{ki}(h_i)}~.
	\end{equation}
	
	Back into Equation~\eqref{eq:SPH_m_dv_dt},
	
	\begin{align}
		m_i \frac{d \mathbf{v}_i}{dt} =& - \sum_{j=1}^{N} m_j \frac{P_j}{\rho_j^2}f_j \left(m_i \grad_i{W_{ij}(h_j)} + \delta_{ij}\sum_{k=1}^{N} m_k \grad_i{W_{ki}(h_i)}\right) \\
		=& - \sum_{j=1}^{N} m_j m_i \frac{P_j}{\rho_j^2}f_j \grad_i{W_{ij}(h_j)} - m_i \frac{P_i}{\rho_i^2}f_i\sum_{k=1}^{N} m_k \grad_i{W_{ki}(h_i)} \\
		\frac{d \mathbf{v}_i}{dt} =& - \sum_{j=1}^{N} m_j \frac{P_j}{\rho_j^2}f_j \grad_i{W_{ij}(h_j)} - \frac{P_i}{\rho_i^2}f_i\sum_{k=1}^{N} m_k \grad_i{W_{ki}(h_i)}
	\end{align}
	
	Renaming index $k$ to $j$ in the second summation, and using the symmetry of the kernel, which implies $\grad_i{W_{ki}(h_i)} = \grad_i{W_{ik}(h_i)}$, we obtain the final form of the equation of motion:
	
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{d \mathbf{v}_i}{dt} = - \sum_{j=1}^{N} m_j\left[\frac{P_j}{\rho_j^2}f_j \grad_i{W_{ij}(h_j)} + \frac{P_i}{\rho_i^2}f_i \grad_i{W_{ij}(h_i)}\right]~.
		\label{eq:SPH_eq_of_motion}
	\end{equation}
	
	\subsection{Equation of energy for SPH particles}
	
	We now derive the evolution equation for the specific internal energy. The total energy of the SPH particles is given by:
	
	\begin{equation}
		E = \sum_{i=1}^{N} m_i u_i + \sum_{j=1}^{N} m_j \frac{\mathbf{v}_j^2}{2}~,
	\end{equation}
	
	and under the condition of energy conservation:
	
	\begin{align}
		\frac{dE}{dt} =& 0 \\
		=& \sum_{i=1}^{N} m_i \frac{du_i}{dt} + \sum_{j=1}^{N} m_j \mathbf{v}_j \frac{d \mathbf{v}_j}{dt}~.
	\end{align}
	
	Now, we use Equation~\eqref{eq:SPH_eq_of_motion} on the right side:
	
	\begin{align}
		\sum_{i=1}^{N} m_i \frac{du_i}{dt} =& -\sum_{j=1}^{N} m_j \mathbf{v}_j\left(- \sum_{k=1}^{N} m_k\left[\frac{P_k}{\rho_k^2}f_k \grad_j{W_{jk}(h_k)} + \frac{P_j}{\rho_j^2}f_j \grad_j{W_{jk}(h_j)}\right]\right) \\
		=& \sum_{j, k} m_jm_k \mathbf{v}_j\frac{P_k}{\rho_k^2}f_k \grad_j{W_{jk}(h_k)} + \sum_{j, k} m_jm_k \mathbf{v}_j\frac{P_j}{\rho_j^2}f_j \grad_j{W_{jk}(h_j)}~.
	\end{align}
	
	Since $j$ and $k$ are dummy indices, we can relabel them to symmetrize the expression. In the first sum we set $j \to i, k \to j$, and in the second sum we set $k \to i$, yielding
	
	\begin{align}
		\sum_{i=1}^{N} m_i \frac{du_i}{dt} =& \sum_{i, j} m_i m_j \mathbf{v}_i\frac{P_j}{\rho_j^2}f_j \grad_i{W_{ij}(h_j)} + \sum_{i, j} m_jm_i \mathbf{v}_j\frac{P_j}{\rho_j^2}f_j \grad_j{W_{ji}(h_j)} \\
		=& \sum_{i, j} m_i m_j \mathbf{v}_i\frac{P_j}{\rho_j^2}f_j \grad_i{W_{ij}(h_j)} + \sum_{i, j} m_jm_i \mathbf{v}_j\frac{P_j}{\rho_j^2}f_j (-\grad_i{W_{ij}(h_j))} \\
		=& \sum_{i, j} m_i m_j f_j \frac{P_j}{\rho_j^2} \mathbf{v}_{ij} \cdot \grad_i{W_{ij}(h_j)}~,
		\label{eq:dt_dt_1} 
	\end{align}
	
	where we have used $\grad_j{W_{ji}(h_j)} = -\grad_i{W_{ij}(h_j)}$ and $\mathbf{v}_{ij} = \mathbf{v}_{i} - \mathbf{v}_{j}$. Since $i, j$ are also dummy indices, we can exchange them, having another solution for the equation of energy:
	
	\begin{align}
		\sum_{i=1}^{N} m_i \frac{du_i}{dt} =& \sum_{i, j} m_i m_j f_j \frac{P_j}{\rho_j^2} \mathbf{v}_{ij} \cdot \grad_i{W_{ij}(h_j)} \\
		=& \sum_{i, j} m_i m_j f_i \frac{P_i}{\rho_i^2} \mathbf{v}_{ji} \cdot \grad_j{W_{ji}(h_i)} \\
		=& \sum_{i, j} m_i m_j f_i \frac{P_i}{\rho_i^2} \mathbf{v}_{ij} \cdot \grad_i{W_{ij}(h_i)}~.
		\label{eq:dt_dt_2}
	\end{align}
	
	Taking the average of Equations~\eqref{eq:dt_dt_1} and~\eqref{eq:dt_dt_2} yields the symmetrized form:
	
	\begin{equation}
		\sum_{i=1}^{N} m_i \frac{du_i}{dt} = \sum_{i, j} m_i m_j \frac{1}{2}\left[f_j \frac{P_j}{\rho_j^2} \mathbf{v}_{ij} \cdot \grad_i{W_{ij}(h_j)} + f_i \frac{P_i}{\rho_i^2} \mathbf{v}_{ij} \cdot \grad_i{W_{ij}(h_i)}\right]~,
	\end{equation}
	
	and the evolution equation for the internal energy is
	
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{du_i}{dt} = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{j=1}^{N} m_j \left[f_j \frac{P_j}{\rho_j^2} \mathbf{v}_{ij} \cdot \grad_i{W_{ij}(h_j)} + f_i \frac{P_i}{\rho_i^2} \mathbf{v}_{ij} \cdot \grad_i{W_{ij}(h_i)}\right]~.
	\end{equation}
